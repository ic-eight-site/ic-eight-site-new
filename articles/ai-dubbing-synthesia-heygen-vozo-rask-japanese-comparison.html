Four AI Dubbing Tools, One Japanese Listener: Only One Required No Japanese Corrections

Cross-product comparison · Published October 2026

Synthesia, HeyGen, Vozo, and Rask AI can all turn an existing English-language video into a Japanese dub that sounds like the original speaker.

That description hides the most important difference between them.

In a same-source comparison, all four systems produced recognizable versions of the speaker's voice. Three produced Japanese that sounded broadly natural. Two produced visible lip synchronization in the tested result. A Japanese speaker reviewed every output. Only one was judged not to require Japanese-language correction in this sample.

For this video, that product was Synthesia.

The question is no longer whether AI can generate a convincing Japanese voice.

It can.

The harder question is what a Japanese-language review discovers after the voice already sounds convincing.

This is not a general ranking across every language, speaker, plan, or video genre. It is the result of one bounded comparison using the same short educational source and one Japanese evaluator.

It nevertheless exposed a distinction that conventional feature tables miss:

A dub can sound natural while becoming less faithful. It can also use an ordinary speaking pace while audible errors make the meaning unreliable.

One source, four different compromises

The source was an approximately 83-second English-language educational video in which a university professor introduced a course about the science and practice of happiness and an app intended to help learners apply those practices in daily life.

The comparison focused on the first 60 seconds wherever the product boundary required it. Rask localized the first minute in its Free Trial. HeyGen initially accepted the full YouTube URL and stated that it would translate the first minute on Free, but the actual Translate action opened a paid-plan gate. Uploading a separately prepared 60-second file succeeded on the same Free account. Synthesia and Vozo had previously processed the longer source.

The dates and account states above come from the individual experiment records; the linked PVEs provide the product-specific context and evidence boundaries behind this later comparison.

The product states were not identical. Synthesia, HeyGen, and Rask were judged primarily on their generated result. Vozo's final listening judgment applies to a corrected version after targeted pronunciation and wording repairs. That difference is part of the result rather than something to hide: it shows how much human work was required to reach an acceptable output.

Six dimensions were reviewed separately: preservation of meaning, naturalness of spoken Japanese, pronunciation and intonation, pace, similarity to the source speaker's voice, and lip synchronization. Pace was interpreted together with meaning retention. A system should not receive credit for sounding comfortably slow if it achieved that pace by removing important information.

Synthesia preserved the meaning and adapted it for listening

Synthesia produced the strongest integrated result.

The Japanese retained the source meaning while avoiding unnecessarily dense Sino-Japanese vocabulary and written-language structures. The output did not merely translate the English sentence by sentence. It reorganized the language so that a listener could understand it from the beginning as it unfolded, without needing to hold a long modifier in memory and wait for the end of the sentence.

This is an important localization skill. Japanese that is defensible on the page may still be tiring or unclear when heard once in real time. Synthesia consistently favored simpler verbs, shorter spoken units, and explicit relationships between ideas.

The pronunciation and intonation were judged effectively flawless in this sample. The pace was easy to follow without sounding slow. The voice remained recognizably close to the original speaker, and the lip sync was visibly present. The combined impression was not of a conventional dubbed track but of the original speaker suddenly speaking fluent Japanese.

Synthesia did retain one source-shaped expression: a version of “And that's where you come in” became 「そこで、皆さんの出番です」. It is understandable, but it sounds more like English discourse logic carried into Japanese than something a Japanese speaker would normally originate in that context.

That did not prevent publication readiness. In this sample, the expression was a localization texture, not a comprehension failure.

The result took approximately 24 minutes to generate and was quoted at 329 credits with lip sync and adaptive duration in a paid Starter account. It was the slowest generation in the comparison, but it was also the only output for which the Japanese-speaking evaluator found no correction necessary. That finding does not demonstrate that future Synthesia outputs can safely be published without Japanese review.

Vozo reached a strong result, but only after repair

Vozo's translation was also strong.

Like Synthesia, it treated the script as language to be heard rather than text to be read. The Japanese was natural, the meaning was preserved, and the voice was recognizably similar to the source speaker. Its overall intonation was encouraging.

The initial output was not ready.

Several words had incorrect or confusing readings. Vozo's pronunciation editor allowed the evaluator to specify kana and regenerate only the affected segments. That successfully repaired readings such as 人 and 名. This is meaningful product value: the user did not need to regenerate the entire video to correct one word.

A pitch-accent problem involving 旅 was harder. Because the visible word and the unintended interpretation could share the same kana reading, pronunciation spelling alone did not solve the problem. The editor did not expose direct Japanese pitch-accent control. The evaluator ultimately rewrote the sentence and regenerated that segment, which produced the correct accent and a substantially better result.

The corrected version was judged broadly acceptable, but it still sounded approximately 1.2 times normal speed to the evaluator. That figure is a perceptual description, not a measured playback-rate value. The important point is the tradeoff: Vozo retained the source content but compressed the Japanese delivery enough to feel rushed.

Lip sync was not evaluated. The account was expected to receive 20 AI points but displayed 10. Lip sync for the source required 16 points, so the test could not be run. This was an insufficient-credit boundary, not an observed finding that the feature was technically unavailable or necessarily restricted to a paid plan.

Vozo therefore reached a useful final output, but not a no-correction workflow. A Japanese speaker had to identify the reading and accent problems, choose the appropriate repair method, rewrite one passage, and confirm the regenerated speech.

The quality judgment applies to Vozo's corrected in-product preview. The Free account allowed preview, editing, and targeted regeneration, but attempting to export the dubbed video or audio opened a paid upgrade gate. An acceptable preview and a downloadable deliverable were therefore separate product outcomes in this test.

HeyGen sounded finished while quietly removing meaning

HeyGen was the most deceptive result in the comparison—not because the Japanese sounded bad, but because it sounded so good.

The generated voice was strikingly similar to the source speaker. The speech was clear, comfortably paced, and easy to understand. Lip sync worked. Minor accent differences, including the rendering of 運動, created only a short recognition delay rather than a comprehension failure. The output completed in approximately five minutes after the exact 60-second source was accepted.

The Japanese itself did not need stylistic rewriting for practical use. More polishing was possible, but it would have risked becoming the kind of discretionary intervention that human translators sometimes perform because they can, not because the audience needs it.

The problem was meaning preservation.

In one passage, the English referred back to “these practices.” The Japanese removed the equivalent of “these,” leaving the listener to wonder which practices were being discussed. A more consequential example came near the end:

I hope it'll make a difference both in terms of teaching you about the science of happiness, but also giving you the tools you need to apply those practices in your daily life.

HeyGen compressed this into a line equivalent to:

I hope it will make a difference in both aspects.

The two aspects themselves—learning the science of happiness and receiving tools to apply the practices in daily life—had disappeared. 「両方の面で」 remained, but the content needed to resolve 「両方」 did not.

This was not an awkward sentence waiting for a better stylistic choice. It was substantive information loss.

HeyGen also produced the best adaptive decision in one conspicuous passage. It was the only tested system that did not preserve the unnatural literal expression 「そこで皆さんの出番です」. Instead, it consolidated the idea into a more natural explanation that the material had been released to reach more people.

That makes the result more interesting than a simple finding of poor translation. HeyGen could make an excellent contextual rewrite in one place and over-compress the meaning in another. Its voice, pace, and lip sync made both decisions sound equally authoritative.

For a publisher who does not understand Japanese, that is the central risk. The output does not announce where it has taken editorial liberties.

Rask translated the workflow more successfully than the speech

Rask had one of the clearest product workflows.

It accepted the public source URL directly, exposed Japanese Voice Clone and speaker autodetection, showed the stages of processing, and returned a segment-based bilingual editor with individual audio previews. The source-to-project path was easy to understand.

The resulting Japanese was not practically usable in this sample.

The audible Japanese felt more like text prepared for the page than speech prepared for the ear. It used heavier Sino-Japanese vocabulary and source-shaped, back-loaded sentence structures that increased the listening burden.

The audible output introduced the more serious failure. 実践 sounded like 時間, turning a journey to learn “the science and practice” into what sounded like a journey to learn “science and time.” No Japanese script was available alongside the audio in this comparison, so it is not possible to determine whether this was a mistranslation or a misreading. 旅 sounded like りつ. イェール lost its long vowel and became close to いえる. ツール became close to つる. Rewire was rendered as something like リワイ. A phrase intended as お役に立てたら became difficult to recover, sounding closer to みなさんのおきにたてたら.

Some of these audible errors could be reconstructed by a Japanese listener who knew the context. Others produced a genuine “What did that mean?” response. The raw speaking rate was ordinary, and the voice resembled the source speaker, but a normal pace could not compensate for meaning-changing output, lost long vowels, incorrect readings, and unnatural learner-like accent patterns.

The initial result was also not lip-synced. Rask offered a separate Lip-sync action, but selecting it in the Free Trial opened a paid-plan path. A real pronunciation correction could be typed into the editor, but applying the regenerated result also opened an upgrade prompt. The product exposed relevant controls without allowing the trial to establish whether they could repair the output.

Rask made the mechanics of localization legible. It did not make the Japanese publishable.

Fluency is not the same as fidelity

The comparison produced three different ways to sound good—or fail to.

Synthesia preserved the content and made it easier to hear.

Vozo preserved the content but delivered it quickly, then relied on a Japanese speaker to repair readings and accent.

HeyGen slowed the experience to a comfortable pace partly by compressing what was said.

Rask used an acceptable speaking rate, but reading-oriented wording and audible errors interrupted comprehension. Because the current comparison was audio-only, it cannot classify every heard error as translation or speech generation.

This is why “natural Japanese” is not a sufficient evaluation category. A publisher needs to ask at least two independent questions:

  1. Does the output sound like plausible Japanese speech?
  2. Does it still communicate the source meaning?

HeyGen passed the first question much more convincingly than the second. Rask's audio-only result did not allow every meaning-changing error to be classified as translation or speech generation. Vozo passed both only after repair. Synthesia was the only system in this sample whose reviewed output required no Japanese-language correction.

What did the Japanese-language review change?

For this one video, the operational conclusion was straightforward.

Synthesia's output was checked by a Japanese speaker, who found no correction necessary for this sample. The evaluation did not test publication without review.

Vozo's corrected version was acceptable, but reaching it required Japanese-language expertise to identify and repair the problems.

HeyGen required a meaning audit. Its surface quality made that audit more important, not less, because the omissions were easy to miss.

Rask was below a practically usable threshold in this output.

That conclusion should not be generalized into a permanent vendor ranking or a claim that Synthesia never needs Japanese review. Video genre may matter. Source speech rate may matter. Sentence structure, proper names, specialist vocabulary, speaker identity, plan tier, and language pair may all change the result.

But a publisher cannot release “average performance.” A publisher releases one specific video.

For that decision, the relevant standard is not whether the platform has millions of users, supports a long list of languages, or can produce a convincing cloned voice.

It is whether the audience can recover the intended meaning—and whether someone still needs to understand Japanese to establish that they can.

Evidence and limits

This comparison used one short English educational video, one visible speaker, one target language, and one human evaluator who understands Japanese. It did not estimate general Japanese error rates or compare multiple genres, accents, speakers, or domains.

The plans and processing ranges differed. Rask and HeyGen were bounded to the first minute in the observed Free workflows. Synthesia used a paid Starter account and processed the longer source with lip sync and adaptive duration. Vozo also processed the longer source, and its quality judgment applies to an in-product preview after targeted human corrections; dubbed-media export was paid-gated. HeyGen used its Speed engine. Rask's dedicated paid Lip-sync result was not generated.

Voice similarity was a human perceptual judgment, not biometric verification. The approximate 1.2-times description for Vozo was not an instrumented speech-rate measurement. “No correction necessary” means that the Japanese-speaking evaluator found no change necessary for this educational sample after review; it does not establish safe publication without review or constitute legal, accessibility, broadcast, or institutional approval.

The comparison does establish a narrower point.

AI dubbing has become good enough that the speaker can appear to cross a language boundary without re-recording. The remaining risk is no longer always obvious robotic speech.

Sometimes the voice sounds finished before the meaning is finished.

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
