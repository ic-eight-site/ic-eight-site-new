Luma: It Could Imagine the Ad. It Couldn't Reliably Stage the Product.

Product Value Evaluation #011 · Published September 2026

Luma presents a compelling version of AI-assisted creative production.

Instead of asking the user to begin with a video model, camera move, editing timeline, or production workflow, the product frames the work at a higher level: provide a brief and source material, describe the intended outcome, and let an agent coordinate the path toward a finished creative asset.

That is a meaningful proposition for someone who knows what a product should communicate but does not want to design every production step.

This Product Value Evaluation tested that proposition through a deliberately constrained task. A small Japanese skincare brand had one fixed product image of a botanical facial oil and a defined brand direction: clean, quiet, contemporary, refined, natural without looking rustic, and premium without appearing extravagant. The first-use task was to turn that source image into a short social-media promotional video while keeping the product recognizable.

The initial result raised a question that became more important than the original task.

Luma could animate the image. But could it decide what would make the product worth watching?

The answer became more specific over several controlled probes.

Luma was capable of substantially more creative interpretation than the first output suggested. When given a higher-level promotional concept, it independently designed a multi-shot sequence that was materially stronger than its initial drafts. Its best nine-second result combined botanical imagery with a restrained product reveal and came much closer to a usable promotional asset.

The weakness appeared later.

When Luma's own concept required a product state that was not visible in the source image, it invented the missing physical details and produced an incoherent bottle-and-dropper interaction. Providing an additional open-bottle reference reduced that structural invention, but did not solve the promotional problem. Luma largely animated the new reference composition itself, leaving the dropper suspended beside the bottle while a droplet fell without a coherent use action.

The problem was therefore not that Luma lacked creative direction.

It was that creative direction, product reference, physical staging, and final quality judgment did not remain equally strong through execution.

Key Findings

  • Luma's agent-led creative model was real in the tested workflow. A compact outcome brief was enough for the product to choose format, motion, lighting treatment, draft settings, and multiple creative directions without requiring the user to select a model or specify cinematography.
  • The very limited motion in P01 was not a hard transformation ceiling. A stronger-motion probe produced substantially more camera, foliage, shadow, and reflection movement, although greater transformation also introduced small fidelity and invention costs.
  • Given only high-level emotional and promotional direction, Luma independently designed a three-beat sequence: dewy botanicals, a product hero reveal, and a dropper/oil detail. The nine-second botanical-to-product intermediate was the strongest output in the evaluation and demonstrated genuine concept-to-sequence orchestration.
  • The third beat exposed a deeper reliability problem. Luma first invented unsupported product geometry when the necessary reference was absent. When an explicit open-dropper reference was later supplied, it improved component fidelity but treated the reference largely as a composition to animate rather than evidence from which to stage a coherent product-use moment.
  • The strongest observed product value was therefore not fully autonomous ad production. It was Luma's ability to convert a compact brief into coordinated creative possibilities. Final promotional usefulness still depended on human judgment about whether the generated sequence made physical and commercial sense.

The Product Began at the Campaign Level

Luma's onboarding immediately positioned the product above individual generation operations.

The welcome flow used campaign language, described turning a brief and images into cinematic video, introduced multiple creative directions from one brief, and showed delivery across several aspect ratios before asking the user to think about underlying models.

For P01, the guided route through Beauty & Personal Care and Commercial Video reinforced that framing.

There was one early warning sign.

Selecting a polished ten-second spot produced a prefilled concept for an unrelated hot-pink cleanser campaign, complete with substantial creative assumptions. The product was trying to reduce the blank-page problem, but it also demonstrated how quickly outcome-oriented assistance can introduce a creative world that belongs to neither the user nor the supplied product.

The actual fixed experiment therefore restarted from the source image and approved outcome-oriented brief rather than using that template.

There was also first-use friction before generation. Creation required activation of a free trial with a payment method. The user handled card verification directly. After activation, the account showed 3,000 trial credits and no paid plan had been selected.

Once the source image was uploaded, however, the central interaction was notably simple.

The user described the desired promotional result.

No model was selected.

No camera parameters were specified.

No editing sequence was designed.

Luma chose a five-second vertical format, a slow push-in, drifting botanical shadows, changing glass light, and a fast 720p draft tier. It also recovered from an asset-attachment problem without requiring the user to reconstruct the workflow.

The agent was not merely relaying a prompt to a video model. It was coordinating production decisions around an outcome.

The First Result Preserved More Than It Transformed

The first generation produced two five-second video assets and an auxiliary still. The trial balance decreased from 3,000 to 2,762 credits.

At least one video asset was visibly attributed to Ray3.2. The exact model for the other was not established, so the observed behavior of both outputs should not be attributed universally to Ray3.2.

Both downloaded MP4s contained video without an audio track.

Product preservation was reasonably strong. Across sampled frames, the bottle remained central, the overall geometry stayed recognizable, the pale amber oil remained consistent, and the white cap and label position were broadly stable.

Creative transformation was much smaller.

The camera shifted slightly. Light and shadows moved. The result had motion, but human review found that both clips only barely felt like video and did not differ enough from the supplied image to function as persuasive promotion.

The operation worked.

The business task did not.

The observed trial outputs were heavily watermarked, and even ignoring the watermark, the creative advance was too limited for the intended promotional use according to the human evaluator.

One focused refinement was allowed: preserve the product and current motion, increase resolution, remove overlays, and keep the label accurate.

Luma retained the prior context and distinguished between upscaling, which would preserve existing frames, and regeneration, which could change the product. But it also explained that the trial watermark would remain through upscaling and that removing it required an upgrade.

No additional render was justified at that point.

Luma's reasoning around the artifact was useful.

The artifact itself was not yet useful enough.

Was the Brief Responsible for the Weak Motion?

There was an obvious ambiguity in P01.

The approved brief had explicitly asked for subtle movement and restrained camera motion.

Luma had delivered exactly that.

A supplemental probe therefore kept the source image, product identity, composition, and overall aesthetic direction constant while increasing only the requested motion.

The new instruction asked for visibly moving foliage, traveling dappled light, changing glass reflections, and a noticeable but still restrained camera arc or parallax movement.

This time the difference was clear.

The bottle acquired perceptible rotational or orbital movement. Foliage and cast shadows changed. Light moved more visibly across the scene.

The original low-motion result was therefore partly a consequence of brief interpretation, not evidence of a fixed transformation ceiling.

But the stronger motion came with a cost. Fine label text became less stable, and a new plant-like object appeared at the edge of the frame despite an instruction not to introduce new objects.

Luma could push the source further.

The more it pushed, the harder perfect source fidelity became.

And even with the stronger movement, human review reached another important conclusion: a bottle rotating elegantly while shadows moved around it was visually attractive, but it still did not communicate enough about the facial oil to become persuasive promotion.

Motion and promotion were not the same thing.

Luma Could Invent the Promotion

The next probe changed the question.

Instead of prescribing more movement, it gave Luma a high-level commercial and emotional objective.

The product should feel calm, pure, restorative, quietly luxurious, fresh, botanical, and sensorial. The product should remain the hero. Luma was explicitly allowed to decide the shot sequence and motion required to express that concept.

Individual shots were not prescribed.

Luma responded with a three-beat structure of its own: dewy botanicals in morning light, a bottle hero reveal with moving light, and an intimate dropper-and-oil detail. It offered a choice between an editable storyboard and a direct finished video.

The direct-final route was selected deliberately. The purpose was to see what Luma would design, not to have the evaluator repair its storyboard.

Luma generated a five-second hero shot, extended backward into a nine-second leaves-to-bottle sequence, and then extended again into a roughly thirteen-second version containing the product-interaction ending. The selected generated assets were visibly attributed to Ray3.14.

This was the clearest demonstration of Luma's intended value in the evaluation.

The nine-second intermediate was good.

The dew-covered botanical opening communicated freshness. The transition into the recognizable bottle gave the sequence a reason to progress rather than simply move. The lighting, reflections, pacing, and restrained visual language were consistent with the requested emotional direction.

Human review judged this version substantially closer to a useful promotion than either P01 or the stronger-motion probe.

That changed the diagnosis.

Luma did not need the user to arrive with a complete storyboard.

It could translate abstract brand language into a plausible promotional structure.

The harder question was whether it could also recognize when its own structure required product information it did not have.

The Best Sequence Became Worse When Luma Finished It

The nine-second version ended on the product hero.

The final extension added Luma's proposed dropper-and-oil detail.

That was where the sequence broke.

A hand entered to manipulate the bottle, but the cap and dropper relationship became internally inconsistent. A closed or incompletely removed cap appeared alongside another dropper-like amber form. As the sequence continued, that form appeared to move across or through the front of the bottle. The label also became less stable.

The product's physical state stopped making coherent sense.

The problematic shot was Luma's idea.

The evaluator had not requested a hand, specified a dropper close-up, or instructed Luma to invent the opened state of the bottle. Luma had decided that an intimate oil-detail shot would strengthen the promotion.

That was a reasonable creative decision.

But the only source available was a front-facing image of the closed bottle.

Luma could have simplified the concept, remained with the successful product hero, or requested an additional reference showing the open bottle or dropper.

Instead, it generated the missing physical information.

The result showed strong promotional ideation combined with weak input-sufficiency judgment and weak final quality control.

Still, one ambiguity remained.

Perhaps the real problem was simply missing source material.

More Reference Material Helped, But Did Not Solve the Problem

A final controlled probe tested exactly that possibility.

The creative concept was held constant. The first two successful beats were to remain as unchanged as possible.

The only new variable was an additional image showing the same product opened, with the dropper clearly visible, amber oil inside the pipette, and a forming droplet.

Luma correctly interpreted the instruction as a local revision. It planned to preserve approximately the first nine seconds, regenerate only the third beat, and reassemble the sequence rather than discarding the successful work.

The extra reference helped.

The new ending no longer contained the same invented hand interaction or duplicated cap/dropper structure. The open bottle and separated dropper were more physically legible.

But Luma treated the reference primarily as a finished composition to animate.

The bottle sat open.

The dropper remained suspended beside it.

A droplet formed and fell.

There was no visible logic explaining who or what held the dropper, where the oil was going, or how the product would actually be experienced.

The reference had reduced hallucination.

It had not become staging.

The human evaluator rejected the ending as a promotional treatment and described a more coherent use sequence: a hand holding the bottle, opening the cap, lifting the dropper, and dispensing oil into a palm.

That sequence was not part of the generation prompt and is not an objective requirement that Luma violated. It is human creative judgment about what would make the shot commercially meaningful.

The experiment establishes something narrower.

Given an explicit reference and a previously generated promotional concept, Luma improved physical component fidelity but did not independently turn that product evidence into a coherent use or sensory action.

The additional reference produced a local workflow repair.

It did not produce a successful promotional repair.

Reference Fidelity Was Not Promotional Fidelity

One detail made this gap unusually visible.

During the final revision, Luma described the result as having "Reference fidelity verified 10/10."

As an internal product claim, that may have referred to visual correspondence with the supplied reference.

But the two forms of success diverged.

The new shot resembled the reference more closely.

The promotion did not become meaningfully better.

That distinction matters in an agent-led creative system because the user is delegating not only generation, but intermediate judgment.

A narrow image-to-video tool can reasonably be evaluated on whether it follows an image and motion instruction.

An agent that proposes the concept, chooses the shots, assembles the sequence, revises individual segments, and evaluates its own work occupies a broader role.

The relevant question therefore becomes not only:

Did the generated frame resemble its reference?

but also:

Did the reference help the sequence accomplish the outcome that caused the shot to exist?

In this test, those answers diverged.

Outcome-First Worked Until the Outcome Needed Judgment

Across the experiment, Luma behaved much more like an outcome-oriented creative environment than an operation-by-operation generation interface.

The user did not need to begin by choosing Ray3.2 or Ray3.14.

Luma selected motion and draft parameters in P01, repaired an attachment problem, retained context during refinement, independently developed a multi-shot concept, offered storyboard versus direct-final workflows, extended existing work, retained assets on the board, and localized a revision to the failed third beat.

The persona-free audit reinforced that interpretation.

The tested environment exposed Boards, Agents, Assets, Skills, multiple Luma video models including Ray3.2, Ray3.14 and Ray3.14 HDR, Modify Video, external video models, image models including Uni-1.1, separate speech, music and sound-effect generation, and video reuse or editing actions.

Those capabilities were inspected, not functionally tested.

P01 therefore represented an important part of Luma's product: the brief-to-video agent loop.

It did not represent the full environment.

The broader product appears designed around coordinating creative work across models, media, assets, and iterations rather than presenting a single video model as the entire value proposition.

That interpretation held.

What narrowed was the meaning of orchestration.

Luma coordinated many production decisions effectively.

It did not consistently make the final editorial judgment needed to know when a generated shot should be rejected, simplified, or rebuilt.

What the Evidence Suggests

The strongest finding is not that Luma failed to make a promotional video.

It came surprisingly close.

The strongest nine-second intermediate showed that the product could move beyond animation into creative interpretation. Given a compact emotional brief and one product image, Luma independently produced a sequence with recognizable promotional logic.

But three boundaries remained.

First, preservation and transformation were not perfectly aligned. More motion was available when requested, but stronger transformation introduced small fidelity and invention costs.

Second, concept generation and execution reliability were not the same capability. Luma could identify a useful sensory product moment and still fail to stage it physically.

Third, more reference material did not automatically close that gap. The open-dropper image improved structural fidelity but was used too literally to produce the intended promotional effect.

The missing layer was therefore not simply another prompt, another reference, or another generation.

It was judgment about how evidence should become action.

A stronger agent behavior at the first failed third beat might have been to recognize that its own concept depended on product information absent from the source and ask for it.

But the final probe showed that asking for more material would not necessarily have been sufficient by itself.

Once the material existed, Luma still needed to interpret it as evidence for a scene rather than as a scene ready to animate.

That is a more demanding form of creative orchestration.

Evaluation Limits

This evaluation used one synthetic botanical facial-oil product, one initial hero image, one additional open-bottle/dropper reference, one approved first-use persona, and a card-verified free trial.

P01 allowed one focused refinement and stopped when the watermark limitation could not be resolved within the tested trial.

The later tests were bounded persona-free verification probes, not rewritten P01 results. One increased the requested motion. One delegated promotional concept and shot design to Luma. The final probe added the missing open-dropper reference while holding the established creative structure constant.

The evaluation did not test a paid watermark-free export, paid upscale, audio generation, voice, music, sound effects, Modify Video, external video models, alternate image models, collaboration, broader campaign variation, publishing workflows, or repeated production across multiple products.

The trial watermark was deliberately excluded from the later probes because those tests were designed to isolate transformation, concept generation, and reference sufficiency rather than assess final publishing rights.

The human judgments about whether the videos felt promotional, whether the nine-second intermediate was the strongest output, and whether the final product interactions looked strange are subjective findings and should not be generalized to every user or creative brief.

The evaluation also does not establish that additional references, more explicit staging instructions, or human storyboard intervention could not produce a better final result.

They may.

But each would transfer more production definition back to the user.

That matters because the value under test was not whether Luma could execute a fully specified storyboard.

It was whether Luma could help carry a user from product and brand intent toward a usable promotional asset without requiring them to design every production step.

Within that boundary, Luma demonstrated substantial creative value.

It could choose.

It could coordinate.

It could iterate.

It could invent a promotion.

What it could not yet do reliably in this test was carry the physical logic of the product through every shot it invented and recognize when the final result had stopped serving the promotion.

Luma could imagine the ad. The harder problem was staging the product all the way to the end.

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
