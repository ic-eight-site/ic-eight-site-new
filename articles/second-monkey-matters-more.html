AI & Value Creation

The Second Monkey Matters More Than the First

A new behavior does not spread because it works. It spreads because someone else notices it working, and says so. · Published August 2026

There is a famous story about Japanese macaques on Koshima Island learning to wash sweet potatoes.

The real story is already interesting enough without the mythology that was later added to it.

One young macaque began washing sand off sweet potatoes in water. Other monkeys gradually picked up the behavior through observation and social contact. Over time, something that had started as one animal's unusual behavior became part of the group's culture.

I keep thinking about this when I look at how people respond to AI.

A new technology does not become normal simply because someone invents it, or even because it works.

At first, the person using it differently often just looks strange.

Why are you doing that?
Why would you let AI do that?
Isn't the old way better?

Then someone else looks at the same behavior without the reflexive rejection and says: actually, that works. I can use that.

That second person matters.

There is another well-known example from a music festival, where one person starts dancing alone. At first, he looks ridiculous. Then a first follower joins him. Soon another joins. Only after that does the behavior begin to look less like one person's oddity and more like something others can participate in.

Where AI adoption actually sits

AI adoption feels a little like that right now.

There is still plenty of resistance, some of it justified and some of it simply habitual. But there are also more people looking at AI without deciding in advance that it is either wonderful or terrible.

They are asking a more useful question:

What can this actually do?

That is where things get interesting.

The people experimenting with AI today are not necessarily trying to replace everything with AI. Often, they are doing something much more mundane: finding specific places where it makes a previously impractical task possible, faster, cheaper, more scalable, or simply different.

In product evaluation, for example, I use AI to inhabit defined user personas and move through the same product experience from different perspectives. In another context, AI role-play can maintain the behavior and language of a fictional character. Elsewhere, it can simulate an interviewer, a customer, or a particular audience cohort.

None of these uses requires believing that AI is universally good.

It only requires noticing when the potato comes out cleaner.

That may be how adoption really spreads.

Not because everyone suddenly becomes convinced.

Because one person tries something unusual, and another person looks over and says:

"Actually… that works."

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
