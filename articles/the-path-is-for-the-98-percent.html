AI & Value Creation

The Path Is for the 98%

Published September 2026

TL;DR: Packaging an AI capability into a usable path has real, demonstrated value. But that value depends on the user's existing workflow. A small minority of people maintain a paid, sustained relationship with a general-purpose AI, and an even narrower group has developed enough fluency to assemble its own path on demand. For that group, a packaged path can be redundant, or even a step backward. For the rest, it is often the more practical way the capability becomes usable — though not always, since free tiers, employer accounts, and embedded AI already reach some of that group too.

A previous piece in this series argued that specialized AI products earn their value not by owning a unique capability, but by turning an available capability into a usable path: an entry point, a set of decisions made legible, a place to inspect and repair the result, a way to carry the task through to something usable.

That argument still holds.

But it left one question unasked.

A usable path for whom?

The Question the Last Piece Didn’t Ask

The earlier argument treated "the user" as a single, general case: someone who could technically get a similar result from a general-purpose AI, but who benefits from having that capability packaged into an entry point, a workflow, and an inspectable result.

That description fits a great many people.

It does not fit everyone.

Some people already have a path. They have spent enough time with a general-purpose AI that they no longer need a product to tell them where to begin, what to enter, or how to inspect the result. They have built that procedure themselves, through repetition, and it lives in how they prompt, not in a product they installed.

For that group, a specialized product is not offering a path. It is offering a path they already have, wrapped in a subscription, a login, and a narrower set of controls than the one they built for themselves.

The interesting question is how common that second case actually is.

Payment data cannot tell us exactly how many people have built their own path. But it does tell us something important about the baseline: personally paying for sustained access to a general-purpose AI is still unusual.

What the Payment Data Actually Shows

Card-transaction analysis from PNC Bank found that roughly 2% of US households pay for a generative AI subscription. Bank of America Institute, looking separately at its own customer data, put the figure at about 3%. Both are far below the roughly 25% of US consumers who pay for a monthly streaming subscription.

That gap is not a gap in awareness. Pew Research found that about half of US adults have used an AI chatbot at least once, and a large share of AI usage now happens through free tiers, employer accounts, or tools with AI quietly built in rather than through a personal subscription bought and renewed on purpose.

So the payment data does not say "most people don’t use AI," and it does not say "98% of people have no path of their own." Free-tier users, employer-provided access, and embedded AI can all produce real fluency without a personal subscription ever appearing on a bank statement. What the data does say, cleanly, is narrower: people who personally maintain a paid, sustained relationship with a general-purpose AI are still a very small minority. That makes it risky to assume the workflows of heavy general-purpose AI users are representative of the broader market — which is the group a specialized product’s path is largely built for.

98% is not a claim that 98% of people need a wrapper. It is a reminder that paid, sustained general-purpose AI use is still exceptional, and that products should not be designed or judged as if it were the norm.

Two Different Things Get Called “Wrappers”

It is worth separating two criticisms that get collapsed into the same word.

The first is a capability critique: this product doesn’t do anything a general-purpose model can’t already do. The previous piece addressed this directly. Owning a unique capability is not the only way to create value; packaging an available capability into a usable path is a legitimate product in its own right.

The second is a redundancy critique, and it is a different claim: this product doesn’t do anything I, personally, can’t already do faster without it. That is not a statement about the product. It is a statement about the person making it, and it is only true for someone who has already internalized the procedure the product is trying to sell.

Both critiques can be correct about the same product, aimed at different people, at the same time.

This distinction matters because it also draws a boundary around which products the argument applies to at all. It says nothing about products whose value sits in a capability that is not widely available outside them — specialized voice, video, or image generation, for instance, where a general-purpose chat model genuinely cannot produce the same underlying output. The redundancy critique only bites when the underlying capability really is something a fluent general-purpose user could already reach on their own.

Where the Path Adds Nothing

For someone who pays for a general-purpose AI subscription and uses it daily across many kinds of tasks, the packaged path can start working against them rather than for them.

A product-specific interface is narrower than an open conversation. It asks for information in its own order, not the order the user already thinks in. It surfaces the controls its designers anticipated, not the ones a specific task actually needs. It creates a second place to remember, a second login, a second thing to check for updates.

None of that makes the product badly built. It makes the product built for a person who does not yet have a faster way to get the same result.

For a heavy general-purpose user, the fastest way to get a form drafted, a document repaired, or a workflow triaged is often to describe the task in a chat window they already have open, in a context that already knows their business, their preferences, and what they asked for last week. A specialized product cannot compete with that on speed, because it is not competing on speed. It is competing on the fact that most people do not have that context, that fluency, or that habit.

A Better Two-Part Question

The earlier piece proposed replacing "can a general-purpose AI do this too?" with "has this product made the capability easier to discover, direct, inspect, and carry through to a usable result?"

That question still needs a second half.

Easier for whom, compared to what they would otherwise do?

For someone without a fluent, self-built path into a general-purpose AI, the honest comparison is often against a blank page, a spreadsheet, a manual process, or no attempt at all — though for someone already comfortable inside a free tier, an employer tool, or an embedded assistant, the comparison may instead be against that lighter-weight habit. Against either baseline, a packaged path is usually a net gain: it replaces something slower or absent.

For the small minority who maintain a paid, sustained relationship with a general-purpose AI and have built real fluency with it, the honest comparison is against a conversation they already know how to have. Against that baseline, the same packaged path has to earn its subscription price by doing something the conversation genuinely cannot: a capability the chat window doesn’t have, a persistent multi-party state a conversation can’t hold, or a record that needs to exist outside the chat entirely.

Both comparisons are legitimate. They just describe different customers, and specialized AI products should not be evaluated only from the perspective of users who already live inside a general-purpose AI workflow.

The path is a real product. It is just not a product for everyone who could, in principle, use it. It is a product for the people who have not yet built one of their own — and paid adoption data is a useful reminder that heavy general-purpose AI users are not yet the default customer.

These observations come from using AI products directly and separating what they promise from what their real workflows make possible.
