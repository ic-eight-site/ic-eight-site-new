Elicit: The Strongest Answer Was the One That Changed

Product Value Evaluation #014 · Published September 2026

Elicit is built for a problem that general-purpose AI can easily make look easier than it is.

A user asks a research question. The system finds papers, synthesizes the evidence, attaches citations, and turns the result into something that can support a decision.

The difficult part is not producing a polished answer.

It is showing what happens when the evidence does not agree.

For this Product Value Evaluation, the fixed first-use task asked whether AI coding assistants reduce software-development task completion time without degrading code quality, reliability, or security, and under what conditions an engineering organization should deploy them.

The approved persona was a technology research or strategy lead who needed a rapid but rigorous evidence brief for an internal deployment decision. The test used a new Basic account and an English interface, and was physically conducted in Brazil with Japan as the target market. This was not recruited-user research or a Japanese localization study.

The initial result was already useful.

One unchanged question triggered four searches, screening of 80 papers, retrieval of 24 of 35 full texts, and analysis of 24 full-text papers. Elicit produced a cited synthesis with five sources and a risk-tiered deployment recommendation.

But the strongest value appeared only after the first answer was challenged.

The evaluator asked what evidence conflicted with the conclusion and explicitly requested that Elicit separate contradiction, uncertainty, and missing evidence.

Elicit found a realistic randomized study in which experienced open-source developers were 19% slower when allowed to use contemporary AI coding tools. It then narrowed its earlier conclusion. It changed the recommendation from risk-tiered augmentation premised on usually faster bounded tasks to a reversible, context-specific trial with explicit stop rules and quality non-inferiority gates.

That correction was the clearest evidence that Elicit was doing something more useful than generating an academic-looking answer with citations.

Key Findings

  • One broad question became a decision-oriented research brief: multi-query retrieval, screening of 80 papers, and a cited five-source synthesis with a risk-tiered deployment recommendation.
  • The strongest value appeared only after Elicit was directly challenged. An explicit request to surface conflicting evidence found a genuine contradictory randomized trial, and Elicit narrowed its recommendation from broad augmentation to a reversible, task-specific trial with stop rules.
  • Traceable was not the same as strong. One security-related source was quoted accurately but was a short, weakly reported study, and Elicit did not make that weakness proportionately visible.
  • Switching from Research Agent to Find papers after the usage limit did not preserve the same evidence set — the free mode's visible results omitted the two pivotal productivity studies that had shaped the earlier answer.
  • In the tested Basic account, further Research Agent messages were disabled immediately after the follow-up that made the first answer more credible, though completed sessions, exports, and Find papers remained usable.

The First Answer Was Useful, but Too Easy to Trust

The first Research Agent response leaned toward a familiar conclusion.

AI coding assistants often improve productivity, but organizations should preserve engineering review, testing, security controls, and risk-based deployment.

The answer cited a controlled GitHub Copilot experiment reporting 55.8% faster completion on a bounded JavaScript task, field evidence suggesting productivity gains, and studies raising quality and security concerns.

At first glance, this was already decision-usable.

The sources were attached.

The reasoning was visible.

The recommendation was proportional enough to sound responsible.

But this was exactly where the evaluation became more interesting.

The existence of citations did not establish that the evidence set was balanced or that the strongest contrary result had been found.

The first answer emphasized positive productivity evidence.

The evaluator therefore asked Elicit to look specifically for conflict.

One Follow-up Changed the Recommendation

The follow-up found evidence that materially altered the interpretation.

A 2025 randomized controlled trial involving 16 experienced open-source developers completing 246 tasks in mature repositories found that allowing AI tools increased completion time by 19%. The participants had expected AI to make them faster and still believed afterward that it had done so. External source verification confirmed that this was a genuine contradictory result in that setting, not merely a different endpoint.

Elicit did not simply append the study as another caveat.

It explicitly tightened the prior conclusion.

The revised answer distinguished three different things:

  • direct contradiction;
  • uncertainty or endpoint mismatch;
  • important evidence that remained missing.

That distinction mattered.

The 19% slowdown study directly contradicted a broad claim that AI assistants usually reduce development time.

By contrast, a study measuring pull requests, accepted suggestions, or perceived productivity was not necessarily contradicting a task-time experiment. It was measuring something else.

Similarly, positive results on bounded coding tasks did not establish preserved reliability, security, maintainability, or total delivery time after review and remediation.

The revised recommendation therefore moved toward local, reversible trials by task type and repository, with an efficiency endpoint that included verification cost and a quality non-inferiority gate.

The product became more credible because its second answer was less confident than its first.

Elicit Was Better at Finding Evidence Than Judging All Evidence Equally Well

The source-level audit supported several of Elicit's headline claims.

The 55.8% speedup in the controlled GitHub Copilot study was traceable to the publication.

The experienced-developer 19% slowdown result was also supported.

A Google engineering experiment reported roughly 21% faster completion in its tested setting, although uncertainty and limited generalizability remained important.

Large field experiments provided useful workplace evidence but did not resolve long-term reliability, maintenance, or security.

The weaker point was evidence calibration.

One security paper used prominently in Elicit's reasoning reported 23.7% more vulnerabilities in AI-assisted code, including higher-severity findings and lower vulnerability-recognition rates.

Those numerical claims were traceable to the paper.

But the paper itself was a short, low-citation publication with sparse reporting. The accessible version did not provide enough detail to independently establish recruitment, random assignment, repository provenance, or raw-data availability. It also relied on short tasks and static analysis.

Elicit repeated the numbers accurately.

It did not make the weakness of the source equally prominent.

That is an important boundary.

Traceable is not the same as strong.

For this task, Elicit worked well as an evidence-discovery and decision-scaffolding system.

It did not eliminate the need for human source appraisal.

The Product Mode Changed the Evidence Set

After the Research Agent allowance was exhausted, Find papers remained available.

The same fixed question returned an exportable ten-row result table and a short five-source synthesis.

That surviving free workflow had real value.

It meant the Basic account still supported discovery after deeper synthesis was blocked.

But the result set was not equivalent.

The visible top ten in Find papers omitted both pivotal productivity RCTs that had shaped the Research Agent answer: the controlled JavaScript speedup study and the experienced-developer slowdown study.

This does not establish that Find papers is generally inferior, nor that Research Agent is comprehensive.

It establishes something narrower and more useful:

the product mode changed the observed evidence set.

That matters because a user who moves from Research Agent to Find papers after hitting the limit is not simply continuing the same workflow with fewer conveniences.

They may be seeing a materially different slice of the literature.

The Basic Limit Arrived at the Worst Possible Moment

The central product-value tension appeared immediately after the strongest result.

The initial answer was useful.

The first adversarial follow-up made it substantially more trustworthy.

Then the observed Basic Agent allowance ended.

The interface showed 100% usage, an October 4 reset date, and a Pro offer at USD 75 per month. It named Research Agent, reports, systematic reviews, and uploaded-source extraction among the workflows requiring more usage.

The exact entitlement rule was not disclosed.

This evaluation therefore does not claim that every Basic account receives exactly two Research Agent turns.

It can only say that in this account and session, one initial answer and one follow-up were completed before further Agent messages were disabled.

That distinction matters because Basic was not empty afterward.

The completed Research Agent session remained readable.

The eight-source table could be inspected and exported.

The answer itself could be exported.

Find papers still worked.

So the problem was not that the free product became useless.

The problem was that the deeper scrutiny loop stopped almost exactly when it had begun to prove its value.

For an evidence product, that is a particularly consequential place to monetize.

The persona's response was not rejection of the product. Reuse intent was high, but immediate subscription intent was low: the specialist value was clear, while the amount of scrutiny enabled by the paid allowance remained unknown. The more plausible next step was to return for another consequential decision after reset, or test Pro when the cost could be assigned to an organizational research project.

First Use Asked for Trust Before Demonstrating Value

The same sequencing issue appeared earlier in onboarding.

Before the product had demonstrated research value, the observed flow foregrounded expensive annual plans.

Skip was not available until a full-name field had been completed.

Those two onboarding states were observed during the session but were not preserved in a durable screenshot.

The saved artifact confirms the subsequent seven-day Pro trial offer, the auto-charge language, the end date, and the two available choices.

The trial stated that the full monthly Pro price would be charged automatically when the trial ended. The trial was declined.

For the tested persona, this produced a strong price and trust reaction before the first research question had even been tested.

That reaction belongs to the simulated persona and evaluator.

It is not evidence that Japanese users broadly reject Elicit, nor that a specific abandonment rate exists.

The safer conclusion is narrower.

For this Japan-targeted first-use lens, monetization appeared before specialist value had been demonstrated.

Later, when the product finally showed why its Research Agent was useful, the same price boundary reappeared through the usage limit.

The two moments reinforced each other.

Why Elicit Was More Than a Generic AI Research Answer

The most product-specific value did not come from prose quality.

A general-purpose AI can also summarize papers and produce recommendations.

What distinguished Elicit in this test was the bundle around the answer:

  • multi-query academic retrieval;
  • visible screening and full-text progress;
  • inline citations;
  • an inspectable source table;
  • persistent sessions;
  • Markdown and CSV export;
  • the ability to challenge a synthesis while preserving its evidence trail;
  • a materially revised recommendation after contrary evidence was requested.

The value therefore sat in the structure around reasoning.

The user could see that the system had searched, screened, retrieved, and synthesized.

They could inspect the cited papers.

They could ask the system to attack its own conclusion.

They could export the resulting evidence.

That made the answer easier to challenge than an unsupported chat response.

It did not make the answer self-validating.

The Broader Product Extends Beyond the Tested Workflow

The later persona-free audit exposed a much broader product.

Visible capabilities included Research Reports, Systematic Review, extraction, Chat with papers, Library, Zotero, Alerts, collaboration and sharing surfaces, API access, a research deck builder, and extensive life-sciences Strategy and Landscape workflows.

None of those capabilities were functionally evaluated here.

Their presence shows product direction, not output quality.

The architecture nevertheless suggests that Elicit is trying to own more of the evidence workflow than paper discovery alone:

question → retrieval → screening → synthesis → source inspection → structured extraction → persistent evidence → reuse or collaboration

This evaluation validated only part of that chain.

The most convincing part was retrieval plus inspectable synthesis plus revision under challenge.

What the Evidence Suggests for the Product

Three opportunities follow directly from the observed behavior.

1. Make contradiction-seeking part of the default evidence loop. The strongest answer appeared only because the evaluator knew to ask what evidence conflicts with this conclusion. That means Elicit already demonstrated the capability to improve a decision through disconfirmation. The opportunity is not to add a generic "be more critical" feature. It is to make the existing research pipeline more visibly challengeable by default — surfacing the strongest supporting evidence, the strongest contradictory evidence, endpoint mismatches, unresolved evidence gaps, and sources with unusually weak provenance. That would turn the behavior discovered in the follow-up into a more legible part of the product's core value.

2. Make evidence strength as visible as citation presence. Elicit did a good job making claims traceable. The next trust layer is to make source quality harder to overlook. The observed security paper shows why: a numerical claim can be quoted accurately while still resting on a weakly reported study. Because Elicit already exposes source tables, study metadata, screening, and full-text retrieval, it has product-specific infrastructure for making this distinction visible. The opportunity is therefore not a generic confidence score. It is clearer evidence calibration within the source-grounded workflow Elicit already provides.

3. Let evaluators complete one credible scrutiny loop before asking them to commit. The tested Basic account demonstrated enough value to make the paid product understandable. But the limit arrived immediately after the first useful challenge to the initial synthesis. For a specialist product at this price point, the relevant question is not only whether a free user will convert. It is whether a researcher, strategy lead, or organization evaluating Elicit can see enough of the product's scrutiny loop — initial synthesis, contrary evidence, revised conclusion, source inspection — to judge whether it belongs in a real research workflow. Allowing evaluators to complete that loop would give them better evidence for an adoption decision without requiring the Basic tier to become an open-ended free research environment.

Evaluation Limits

This evaluation used one fixed research question concerning organizational deployment of AI coding assistants.

The approved persona was simulated. No recruited users participated.

The test was physically run in Brazil, targeted Japan, and used an English interface. It was not a Japanese localization study and does not establish Japanese market behavior, willingness to pay, or adoption rates.

The Research Agent result does not establish that Elicit finds all important counterevidence automatically. The pivotal contradictory study appeared after an explicit conflict-seeking follow-up.

The exact Basic usage formula was not disclosed, so the observed two-turn pattern should not be generalized to every Basic account.

Source verification established that major headline figures were traceable to their publications. It did not validate every study design, conclusion, or dataset.

Positive task-completion results should not be interpreted as proof that quality, security, maintenance, or long-term reliability were preserved.

The 19% slowdown RCT is genuine contrary evidence in one realistic setting, not evidence that AI coding assistants generally slow developers.

The visible top ten in Find papers omitted two pivotal studies from the Research Agent evidence set, but this evaluation did not measure recall, ranking quality, or completeness across either mode.

The annual-plan framing and the inability to skip before entering a full name were observed during onboarding but were not preserved in a durable screenshot.

Reports, Systematic Review, Extract data, Chat with papers, deck building, Alerts, API, collaboration, Zotero, browser extension behavior, MCP, file or public-data analysis, charts and figures, and life-sciences Strategy and Landscape workflows were visible or signaled but not functionally tested.

Paid-plan value, behavior after a fresh quota reset, and long-term retention remain unknown.

No claim should be made from this evaluation about all research questions, users, languages, or account types.

But for the narrow question tested, the result was unusually clear.

The first answer was useful, and easy to trust too quickly.

One follow-up made it more trustworthy by making it less confident.

And the deeper scrutiny that had just proven its worth was the thing the free tier could no longer afford to give.

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
