Recraft: The Vector Is Real. The Workflow Is Still Yours.

Product Value Evaluation #008 · Published August 2026

Recraft presents itself as more than an AI image generator.

Its current positioning emphasizes design-oriented generation, creative control, editable assets, true vector output, and production use. Vector generation is especially prominent: Recraft states that its vector models produce actual SVG files with editable geometry rather than raster images that merely look like vectors.

That creates a more demanding Product Value Discovery question than whether Recraft can generate an attractive illustration:

Can a first-time user move from a fixed creative brief to a genuine, editable vector asset that can continue into later design work?

The test represented a person in Japan running a small organic soap brand without a dedicated illustrator or designer. The task was to create one reusable vector illustration for a spring campaign: a central bar of soap, botanical elements, and bubbles or soft foam, with a clean, light, natural, soft, contemporary direction. The deliverable was an asset, not a finished advertisement, package, mockup, or social post. No reference image was supplied.

The central claim held.

Recraft generated a genuine path-based SVG, exposed palette editing and individual vector-shape access inside the product, and allowed the asset to be exported from the Free plan.

But the broader interpretation narrowed.

Recraft successfully produced an editable vector asset. It did not demonstrate an end-to-end path from idea to guaranteed production-ready outcome. The product supplied integrated design operations, while the user still had to choose, maintain, and police the workflow.

Key Findings

  • Recraft's central true-vector proposition held. A first-time user generated a vector illustration with Recraft V4.1 Vector, edited its palette, accessed an individual vector shape, and exported a genuine path-based SVG rather than a raster image wrapped in an SVG container.
  • One generation operation produced two visibly different candidates that both followed the structural brief. Selecting the stronger result still required human judgment about hierarchy and requirement coverage.
  • Recraft integrated generation, vector editing, palette adjustment, shape selection, and export inside one canvas, but the user still had to identify and maintain the correct path through the product.
  • A first-use mode change exposed that boundary. Selecting an apparently relevant style changed the active workflow from Vector back to Image, and returning to Vector cleared the prompt. The user had to recognize and recover from the change manually.
  • Structured vector export was demonstrated, but full production readiness was not. External-editor interoperability, path organization, print behavior, production handoff, originality, audience response, and commercial deployment were outside the evidence established by this experiment.

The Core Vector Claim Held

The most important finding was also the simplest.

Recraft did not merely generate an image that looked like vector artwork.

The tested route moved from the initial Chat-oriented workspace into Create. The output type was changed from Image to Vector, and Recraft automatically selected Recraft V4.1 Vector. One generation operation returned two square alternatives. Both kept the soap bar central, included botanical forms, and represented bubbles or foam. Neither introduced text or turned the task into a finished advertisement.

The stronger candidate was then inspected beyond its appearance.

Recraft labeled the result as Vector. Its color controls exposed 22 swatches. Applying a palette preset visibly recolored the illustration without requiring regeneration, and reset restored the original generated palette.

Double-clicking the illustration entered a Vector shape mode. The central soap form could be selected individually and its color inspected.

The asset was then exported as SVG.

The downloaded file was 71,839 bytes and contained 141 <path> elements. No <image>, data:image, or raster-reference href was detected.

That distinction matters.

A raster image can look like vector artwork while remaining a flat grid of pixels. Recraft's output retained structured vector geometry that could be accessed and modified as vector material.

For the narrow first-use task, that is meaningful product value.

The user did not end with an attractive preview that would first need to be recreated elsewhere as vector material.

The generated output was already a structured SVG.

One Generation Was Enough for the Fixed Task

The experiment deliberately avoided repeated regeneration simply to search for a preferred aesthetic.

Both first-generation candidates followed the structural requirements. They differed visibly in density, plant forms, color distribution, and negative space rather than appearing as near-duplicates.

The left candidate was selected because it made the brief more explicit: the soap remained clearly dominant, leaves and small floral forms were present, and multiple bubbles or foam elements were easy to identify.

That does not establish that the left illustration was objectively better.

It establishes only that it covered the fixed requirements more clearly. Audience preference, commercial performance, originality, and aesthetic superiority were not tested.

The AI therefore did not eliminate the design decision.

Instead, it converted a text brief into two editable design candidates in one generation operation, allowing the next human decision to begin from concrete visual material rather than a blank canvas.

Design Controls Were Visible Early

Before generation, Recraft surfaced controls for model, style, references, ratio, and colors.

The Style library included options such as Clean Neutral and Painted Flatstyle alongside many more specialized visual directions. These controls made design choices visible before generation.

They also left the user responsible for choosing the relevant output mode and visual direction.

No design terminology prevented completion, but understanding the distinction among Image, Vector, Vectorize, style references, and the broader style library required some design literacy.

Onboarding had already recorded selections indicating an interest in Vector art, Product visuals, and Marketing / Advertising assets.

The explicit text-to-vector route found in this test, however, was in Create rather than the initial Chat surface. The user opened the output-type control, changed Image to Vector, and Recraft then selected the corresponding vector model automatically.

The experiment did not test whether the same outcome could also have been reached directly from Chat.

The evidence is therefore about the route that was observed, not every possible route through the product.

A Small Mode Change Revealed a Larger Workflow Boundary

The most revealing friction occurred before the first generation.

With Vector selected, the user chose the apparently relevant Clean Neutral style.

The interface then changed the active output from Vector back to Image and replaced the vector model with the general image model.

Returning to Vector cleared the entered prompt.

No credits were consumed, and the problem was recoverable.

But there was no visible warning or explanation establishing why the change occurred. The experiment therefore supports only the observed behavior: selecting that style did not preserve the active vector workflow. It does not establish that Clean Neutral is technically incompatible with Vector.

The incident was small, but its Product Value implication was larger.

The user had already chosen an output type: Vector.

A later design choice changed that output mode.

The user therefore had to monitor whether the product was still operating inside the intended workflow.

That is an important boundary between an integrated design workspace and a system that takes responsibility for preserving the task definition across intermediate decisions.

Editing Continued Without Regeneration

Post-generation editing showed a stronger form of workflow continuity.

A temporary palette change recolored the existing vector from its original palette to a dark neutral and red palette.

The operation modified the selected vector rather than generating a replacement.

Reset restored the original palette.

Individual shape access provided another useful signal. The central soap form could be selected directly in Vector shape mode.

The experiment did not continue into detailed anchor-point manipulation, and it did not test whether every leaf, bubble, or botanical form had a conveniently editable semantic structure.

But it established that the illustration was not an indivisible surface.

At least part of the generated design remained accessible as vector geometry inside Recraft.

This is where Recraft's value extends beyond generation.

If every change requires another prompt and another image, the workflow remains close to conventional AI image generation.

Here, at least some downstream changes could be made to the existing asset itself.

Generation became the beginning of a design process rather than necessarily the end of one.

Integrated Does Not Mean Self-Coordinating

Recraft brought several relevant operations into one environment.

The user could choose a vector mode, generate candidates, select one, adjust colors, enter vector-shape editing, and export an SVG without leaving Recraft Studio.

It also reduced one model-level decision automatically: selecting Vector caused Recraft to choose Recraft V4.1 Vector.

But the observed route still required important user decisions.

The user moved from the initial Chat-oriented surface to Create, distinguished Vector from Vectorize, selected Vector, recognized and recovered from the unexpected mode change, selected the candidate that best covered the brief, and deliberately inspected editing and export controls.

The evidence therefore supports a specific distinction:

Recraft behaved as an integrated creative workspace more strongly than as a self-coordinating design agent.

Whether creative professionals prefer this balance of explicit control and workflow responsibility was not tested here.

What the experiment does establish is where responsibility remained.

Recraft reduced the work required to create editable vector material.

The user still determined which workflow to use, whether the product remained in that workflow, which candidate met the brief, and whether the resulting asset had the properties needed for the next stage.

"Production-Ready" Requires More Than a Valid SVG

The exported SVG is important evidence.

It establishes that the asset was not simply an image-generation result trapped inside Recraft.

The file passed XML validation, contained path-based vector geometry, and could be downloaded from the product.

But that is not enough to call the asset unconditionally production-ready.

The experiment did not import the SVG into an external professional vector editor.

It did not inspect whether all 141 paths were logically grouped or named.

It did not evaluate detailed anchor-point quality.

It did not send the asset to print or test it inside a real brand or production system.

Structured vector export is therefore better understood as a necessary precondition for production use.

It is not proof of every downstream production condition.

The Free plan introduced another boundary.

During this session, Recraft's plan screen stated that Free outputs were public and could not be used commercially. The generated asset was sufficient for evaluating Recraft's technical value as a design object, but this particular Free-plan output could not simply become the soap brand's live marketing asset under the observed plan conditions.

Operation success and commercial deployment were therefore separate questions.

The Product Value Became More Specific

The initial public-positioning interpretation was broad:

Recraft appeared to offer a path from an idea to a controllable, editable, production-ready visual asset, with true vector generation and downstream SVG use as important differentiators.

After direct use, the strongest part of that interpretation remained intact.

Recraft generated an asset in an explicit vector mode, allowed palette-level editing and individual-shape access, and exported real path-based SVG geometry.

The central true-vector proposition held.

What narrowed was the broader workflow interpretation.

The experiment did not establish a complete idea-to-production path in which Recraft independently preserved the intended mode, chose the required operations, validated the asset, and delivered a guaranteed production-ready result.

A more precise statement of the demonstrated first-use value is:

Recraft can turn a fixed creative brief into genuine editable vector material inside an integrated design environment, provided the user chooses and maintains the correct workflow.

That is narrower than "AI takes an idea all the way to production."

It is also substantially more than "AI generates a nice-looking picture."

What the Evidence Suggests for the Product

The experiment points to three product questions.

1. Preserve the user's chosen output contract. Once a user explicitly selects Vector, subsequent design choices should make any change in output mode highly legible. Switching from Vector to Image changes the nature of the deliverable, not merely its appearance. The user should not have to discover that change after another control has silently altered the active workflow.

2. Use onboarding intent to reduce workflow discovery. Recraft had already recorded that this user selected Vector art, Product visuals, and Marketing / Advertising assets during onboarding. That information could potentially make the most relevant creation route easier to surface. The experiment did not establish that Chat could not perform the same task. The opportunity is narrower: use known creation intent to make the relevant available routes more legible from first use.

3. Make the boundary of "production-ready" easier to evaluate. Recraft successfully demonstrated structured vector continuity. The next question for a professional user is what happens after export. External interoperability, grouping, editability in downstream tools, commercial rights, and production constraints are separate from the fact that an SVG is structurally valid. The experiment does not establish that Recraft fails those later requirements. It establishes that they remain additional questions after the first successful vector generation.

Evaluation Limits

This evaluation used one fixed creative brief, one prompt-based generation operation producing two candidates, one temporary palette edit, one individual-shape inspection, and one SVG export.

It did not test paid features, multiple models, custom styles, reference images, APIs, collaboration, batch production, external vector editors, print production, or real commercial deployment.

It also did not test intellectual-property originality, consumer preference in Japan, campaign performance, objective aesthetic quality, or whether the generated illustration would outperform another asset.

Recraft also exposes broader creative pathways beyond the scope of this task, including video-related workflows. Those were not tested here.

The conclusion is deliberately narrow:

Recraft's true-vector claim held. The product generated and exported genuine vector geometry and provided meaningful in-product editing controls. What narrowed was the larger interpretation around workflow orchestration and production readiness.

Recraft did not merely generate an image.

It generated material that could continue to be designed.

The vector was real. The workflow was still the user's.

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
