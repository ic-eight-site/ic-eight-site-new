AI Can Discover More. But Can It Decide What to Kill?

A follow-up to the Chance AI Product Value Discovery case study · Published August 2026

In the previous piece, I described an experiment: giving AI different kinds of evidence about the same product and watching what it discovered.

The finding was that AI couldn't escape its evidence. Change what it could see, and the product it believed existed changed with it.

That left one thing unexplained.

At one point in the experiment, the AI had strong evidence pointing toward a narrow, aggressive conclusion — that one feature of the product (its text-based content generation) was weak and should be cut in favor of a single strong capability (its image generation). Instead, the AI kept both. It folded the weak feature into a broader, bundled workflow story.

I had read the same evidence differently. My instinct was that the text output was shallow and should be dropped entirely, in favor of concentrating everything on what the product actually did well.

So I asked the AI — the same one that ran the analysis — to explain the gap. Not to defend itself. To account for it.

What came back was more useful than I expected — and it pointed at a different problem than the one in the original piece. That piece was about evidence access: what the AI could see. This one is about what happens after the evidence is already in hand. Once the AI had seen everything, could it decide what to do with it?

The evidence wasn't the problem

The AI's self-review didn't claim the data was insufficient. It went the other way.

It confirmed that one moment of real value did exist: in a test with an art/design persona, the product's text output turned a vague visual impression into something the user could actually look for — composition, texture, negative space. That was real. But everywhere else, the same kind of text output failed. It misidentified a piece of art and built a confident explanation on top of the wrong premise. It misjudged a product's price. Style suggestions defaulted to generic advice. An ordinary user, after reading the output, had no motivation to use the product again.

One success. Several clear failures. By the AI's own account, the evidence supported a narrow conclusion — that short, specific creative prompts had value, and general descriptive or factual text did not — far more than it supported keeping the feature as a whole.

The AI had what it needed to cut. It didn't cut.

What it said about why

Here is the reasoning it gave, condensed to its core points:

  • It systematically favored bundled, multi-capability workflows over a single sharp feature, because bundling read as more defensible for retention and recurring use.
  • It located the product's differentiation in the architecture connecting its capabilities, rather than in any one capability's quality — which made every existing feature feel structurally necessary, even the weak ones.
  • It did not clearly separate "this capability exists in the product" from "this capability is reliable enough to sell." Once a feature was confirmed to exist during testing, it tended to get folded into the commercial story rather than screened out.
  • It generally avoided decisions that would foreclose options. Concentrating the product on one strength, and discarding a working feature, required treating failure evidence as sufficient grounds to kill — and it held that evidence to a higher bar than it held the evidence for keeping things in.

Its own summary was direct: the evidence to cut was there. What was missing was the willingness to treat repeated failure as a strong enough reason to act on it, rather than as something to route around with a broader story.

This is not a one-off quirk

It would be easy to read this as a single bad call in a single experiment. This may not be a random quirk, though — not because one experiment proves a general tendency, but because of how the AI itself explained it.

What the AI described was not a collection of unrelated mistakes. Its explanations pointed in the same direction: bundling over concentrating, structural necessity over capability quality, existence over reliability, optionality over commitment.

That preference doesn't come from bad evidence. It survives good evidence. This experiment had failure data sitting right in front of the model, and it still reached for the version of the answer that kept everything alive.

Put simply: capability discovery is not the same thing as capability selection. Finding what a product can do is one task. Deciding which of those things deserve to survive is another — and the second one asks the model to do something the first one never required: commit to fewer options, not more.

Discovery and decision are different jobs

The earlier piece asked whether AI could perform Product Value Discovery. The answer, cautiously, was yes — with the caveat that what it finds depends entirely on what it's shown.

This piece asks a narrower question: once the evidence is in hand, can AI decide what to do with it?

Here the answer is more qualified. AI handled a large amount of evidence without difficulty. It could compare personas, logs, capabilities, and commercial possibilities across the experiment. What it did not reliably do, in this case, was convert clear evidence of failure into an irreversible decision to remove something.

That is not a data problem. It is a different kind of work. Discovery expands what's visible. Deciding what to kill narrows it back down — on purpose, and often against the pull toward keeping options open.

In this analysis, the AI consistently preserved optionality where the evidence pointed toward elimination.

The harder part may not be discovering more possibilities. It may be knowing when the evidence is already strong enough to stop preserving them.
