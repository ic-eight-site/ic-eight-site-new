Visily: When a Product Concept Becomes a Shared Visual Object

Product Value Evaluation #029 · Published September 2026

Visily is positioned as a pre-development UI design and prototyping environment. Its public proposition is not that it will turn an idea into production software, but that a prompt, screenshot, sketch, or diagram can become an editable interface that people can refine, connect into a prototype, present, and share.

That distinction shaped this Product Value Evaluation.

The test did not ask whether Visily could build a functioning workforce-management SaaS. It asked whether a reasonably specific product concept, expressed in Japanese, could become a coherent visual artifact quickly enough to support product discussion before implementation.

The result was strong in one important respect.

Visily turned a Japanese description of a workforce-management product into a coherent four-screen B2B SaaS concept without requiring detailed specifications for spacing, color, typography, grids, component libraries, widths, or navigation structure. The first Dashboard preview was ready in 194 seconds. The next three screens, Shift Planner, Shift Requests, and Settings & Budget Control, took 111, 116, and 136 seconds respectively.

Before intentional editing, the four screens already looked and read like parts of the same product.

That was the most important value discovered in the session.

The prototype was not functionally complete or internally consistent, but it made an abstract product idea concrete enough to inspect, challenge, and discuss.

Key Findings

  • Japanese product requirements became a coherent four-screen SaaS concept without professional design specifications. Dashboard, Shift Planner, Shift Requests, and Settings shared enough structure, vocabulary, navigation, and visual language to read as one product before intentional editing.
  • Auto Prototype created a plausible flow map but did not produce reproducible Dashboard navigation in Presenter. Expected interactions did not move the presentation away from “1 of 4.”
  • Visual completeness hid unresolved inconsistencies. The untouched Dashboard mixed English and dollar-denominated labor costs into a Japanese/yen interface, and the dominant graphic was identified as a “Real image.” Generated dates also conflicted across screens.
  • Direct editing and focused Japanese AI revision both worked, but demonstrated different things. One manual date edit persisted, while the AI revision added the requested budget-risk warning without correcting unrelated defects.
  • Object hierarchy affected downstream prototyping. The AI-revised Dashboard existed as a Container rather than a full Screen, which prevented it from joining the same four-Screen Auto Prototype flow without further restructuring.
  • Collaboration value remained plausible rather than demonstrated. Share and comment controls were visible, but no second participant, invitation, permission flow, or comment exchange was tested.

The First Generation Understood More Than Japanese Labels

The first Dashboard prompt was written entirely in Japanese and described a workforce-management product called ShiftSmart.

The generated result contained the three requested KPI concepts, store-level staffing status, five pending requests, bulk approval, and per-row actions. It was recognizably a manager-facing operational dashboard rather than a generic analytics layout.

The useful result was not simply that Japanese text appeared in the interface. The screen reflected a broader interpretation of the product's purpose: labor-cost monitoring, staffing shortages, pending shift requests, and managerial intervention.

The remaining screens extended that interpretation. Shift Planner presented week controls, an auto-scheduling action, staff-by-day scheduling, staffing-gap indicators, and labor alerts. Shift Requests became an operational approval queue. Settings & Budget Control connected labor-cost thresholds and notification preferences with historical context.

Before editing, the four screens already shared navigation, entities, terminology, and enough visual language to read as one product.

This was stronger evidence of product understanding than translated interface chrome. Written requirements had become something that could be shown to another person and discussed as a product rather than described only in words.

Visual Coherence Did Not Mean Internal Consistency

The same first generation also showed why a convincing prototype still needs inspection.

The Dashboard's most visually dominant chart switched to English and displayed dollar-denominated labor costs inside an otherwise Japanese and yen-denominated interface. Later verification identified that graphic as a Real image, rather than establishing it as a native or data-backed chart.

There was also a temporal inconsistency across screens. Shift Planner used the requested September 2026 timeframe, while Dashboard, Shift Requests, and Settings contained invented May 2024 records.

These defects did not stop the screens from looking coherent at a glance. That is why they matter.

A sufficiently polished generated artifact can make illustrative data, inconsistent dates, or embedded pseudo-data elements feel more resolved than they actually are. The first-generation result was useful as a product-conversation artifact, not evidence that all underlying product decisions had been completed.

Direct Editing and AI Revision Solved Different Problems

The evaluation separated direct editing from AI-assisted revision.

One generated submission date in Shift Requests was manually changed from 2024/05/18 to 2026/09/07, and the edit persisted. That established that direct editing worked in this test without requiring the screen to be regenerated.

It did not normalize the remaining invented dates and should not be interpreted as doing so.

A separate Dashboard variant was then created through a Japanese AI revision request. The original forecast labor cost of ¥4,285,000 had no prominent indication that it represented a budget risk. The evaluator asked Visily AI to add a warning near that KPI. Around 90 seconds later, the revised variant included a visible 予算超過の見込み treatment while preserving the original Dashboard separately.

Before and after the focused Japanese AI revision. The revised Dashboard adds a yellow “予算超過の見込み” warning near the forecast labor-cost KPI while unrelated issues remain.

The revision improved the specific weakness it was asked to address. It did not repair unrelated issues: the English/dollar chart remained, and the May 2024 request data remained.

That separation is important when evaluating AI editing. A successful targeted revision demonstrates that one requested change worked. It does not improve the assessment of untouched parts of the artifact.

The revision also exposed a structural distinction that later affected prototyping. The new Dashboard variant was added as a Container, not as a full Screen.

Visually, that difference was easy to miss. Structurally, it mattered.

Auto Prototype Made the Flow Easier to See Than to Run

The natural command-search path did not expose Auto Prototype during first use.

In the later persona-free audit, Visily's in-product guidance showed that whole Screen objects had to be selected in Prototype mode before Create prototype with AI became available. Selecting inner containers was insufficient. Because the AI-revised Dashboard existed as a Container rather than a complete Screen, it could not simply replace the original Dashboard in the four-screen prototype selection without further restructuring.

Once the original four Screens were selected correctly, Auto Prototype created a flow named Enterprise Shift & Labor Management Suite in 54 seconds. The generated map connected Dashboard to Shift Planner, Shift Requests, and Settings in plausible ways. The observed AI-credit balance moved from 480 to 470 during this step.

As a visual representation of intended flow, the result was useful. The connections made the product easier to discuss without manually wiring every screen first.

The map was not treated as proof that the navigation actually worked.

Presenter listed all four screens, yet the Dashboard's white left-navigation labels disappeared against the dark rail. Clicking the expected navigation regions, 新規シフト作成, the pending-request KPI, and 詳細分析 did not move the state away from 1 of 4.

The 詳細分析 control also had no valid destination in the tested structure because no detailed-analysis screen had been requested or generated.

The supported conclusion is therefore narrower than “Auto Prototype failed.” It successfully created a named, visually plausible connection map. What the evaluation could not reproduce was working Dashboard navigation through the expected routes in Presenter.

For a prototyping product, that distinction matters. Visily reached an editable and presentable shared artifact more convincingly than it reached a reliably runnable prototype.

The Strongest Value Was Making the Concept Discussable

Before generation, ShiftSmart existed as written requirements. After generation, there was a Dashboard, Planner, Requests queue, and Settings surface that could be inspected together.

That changed the kind of conversation the concept could support.

Instead of discussing whether budget visibility should exist in the abstract, someone could point to the Dashboard and ask whether the forecast warning was prominent enough. Instead of describing a workflow verbally, a teammate could look at the generated screens and identify a missing action or request another screen.

The evaluator's own first-use reaction emphasized this value: the artifact could be shown to someone else and used to ask, in practical terms, これどうよ？ and then collect concrete reactions or requests for changes.

This is not evidence that Visily's collaboration system itself works well. Invitations, permissions, comments, and a second participant were not exercised.

The demonstrated value is simpler. Visily reduced the cost of turning a product idea into something concrete enough for shared product discussion.

Japanese Mattered at the Product-Structure Level

Because the prompts were in Japanese, the experiment also produced a useful localization observation.

The important result was not merely that Visily generated Japanese labels. It interpreted Japanese product requirements well enough to create a recognizable operational workflow across multiple screens.

The evaluator described the resulting interface as substantially easier to understand once the AI had interpreted the product and its purpose. That remains a human judgment rather than a general market claim.

Still, it points to a distinction relevant to AI-native design tools. Language understanding can affect product structure, not only interface copy.

Visily showed that strength at the concept level. It did not propagate the same contextual consistency into every detail: currency, chart language, invented dates, object hierarchy, and prototype destinations remained imperfect.

The opportunity is therefore not simply better Japanese localization. It is better continuity between the product understanding demonstrated during generation and the details that determine whether the artifact can be trusted.

Implications for Visily

The evaluation suggests that Visily's strongest value lies in accelerating product alignment before implementation.

Its AI generation already did a good job of turning a Japanese product concept into a coherent visual system. A useful next step would be to make the artifact's apparent completeness correspond more closely to what has actually been verified.

A prototype-audit layer could flag inconsistent currencies and dates, distinguish illustrative images from native data components, identify controls whose destination screens do not exist, and validate expected prototype routes before presentation. The Screen/Container distinction could also be surfaced more clearly when it affects downstream behavior.

These are product-specific opportunities because Visily owns the design artifact, its object hierarchy, AI revisions, and prototype graph.

The broader implication is that the same AI that makes an early product idea easy to visualize could also help distinguish between elements that have become real structural decisions and those that remain illustrative assumptions.

Evaluation Limits

This evaluation used one simulated representative first-use lens rather than recruited-user research.

Visily was evaluated as a UI design and prototyping environment, not as a production application builder. The absence of real scheduling persistence, labor-cost calculations, authentication, notifications, integrations, or production deployment is therefore not treated as failure.

The initial Dashboard preview appeared in 194 seconds, and the three later screen generations took 111, 116, and 136 seconds. The supported credit checkpoints were 570 after the Dashboard, 480 after the subsequent three-screen sequence, unchanged at 480 after the AI revision, and 470 after Auto Prototype. The balance before the initial generation sequence was not observed, so total cost from account creation cannot be calculated.

The evaluation does not establish that every generated component was natively editable, that generated labor figures or staffing alerts were internally calculated, or that Auto Prototype created functioning Dashboard routes. Real data binding, accessibility, responsive behavior, collaboration, export and handoff fidelity, integrations, long-term persistence, and repeat-generation variance remain untested.

Conclusion

Within those limits, the central result was clear.

Visily turned a written Japanese product concept into a coherent visual artifact quickly enough to change the nature of the conversation around it. Its strongest demonstrated value was not that it produced a finished product, but that it produced something concrete enough for people to inspect, revise, and discuss before that product existed.

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
