Positioning & Assumptions

When You Build a New Category, AI Rounds You Down to an Old One

A category that doesn't exist yet in the training data gets absorbed into the nearest one that does — even when the page explicitly says it isn't that. · Published August 2026

For a few months this year, IC Eight had a name for one part of its work: the AI-Native Localization Framework.

The idea behind it was specific: generate Japanese product content from intent, product context, and constraints, not by translating an English source sentence by sentence. It was a real distinction, and it mattered to the work.

Then the positioning shifted. The Framework stopped being the company's primary positioning and became a specialized methodology, used only when a diagnosis points to the content system as where value is being lost. Most of the time, that isn't where the problem is. The new language, Product Value Discovery and Product Value Diagnosis, went up across the site, explaining a broader diagnostic approach that the Framework was never meant to represent on its own.

And AI kept describing IC Eight as a Japanese localization company.

Not maliciously. Not because anyone typed the wrong thing. Because of how the earlier positioning was still structured underneath the new one.

The category AI reached for wasn't wrong. It was just the nearest one it had a lot of data for.

Japan market entry, localization, and cultural adaptation are all well-populated categories. They come with common enough associations that a model can produce a confident-sounding paragraph about them with very little actual signal. "Product Value Discovery" is not a populated category. It's one company's term for a specific diagnostic approach. When a model has almost nothing to go on, it doesn't leave the space blank. It reaches for the nearest thing that looks similar enough, and fills in the rest from what's common in that neighborhood.

This showed up in three separate ways, and each one taught something different.

First: a correct definition at the top of the page can still lose to a denser familiar pattern below it

IC Eight's About page opened with the actual claim, a plain statement of the gap the company exists to diagnose. That part was already right. But the paragraphs that followed described years of work on Japanese product language for well-known technology companies. That biography was accurate and relevant. It was also dense with exactly the vocabulary that maps cleanly onto the familiar category: "Japanese," "language," recognizable brand names. A model weighing the page didn't need the biography to come first to be pulled toward it; it only needed that section to carry a stronger, more recognizable signal than the one sentence stating the actual claim.

The fix wasn't to hide the biography. It was to make sure the opening claim did more work on its own, and to let the biography read clearly as supporting background rather than as competing evidence for a different conclusion.

Second: negation may not neutralize a category signal as cleanly as a human writer expects

Writing "we do not assume this is a localization problem" still puts the word "localization" on the page, attached to the company. A human reader parses the negation without effort. What this case suggests, not as a general rule but as one observed instance, is that the rejection didn't travel with the word as reliably as expected. The term surfaced in descriptions of the company more often than the sentence rejecting it did.

The more reliable fix was removing the term where it wasn't load-bearing, and stating what the work actually does instead of relying on stating what it doesn't do.

Third: internal structure teaches the concept, whether or not that's the intent

Twenty-seven separate pages linked to the Framework article at the time, not because anyone decided to build a shrine to it, but because it had been the natural "learn more" destination for a whole cluster of related essays when they were written. Individually, each link made sense. In aggregate, it made a now-secondary methodology one of the most heavily reinforced ideas on the entire site, well after the primary positioning had moved elsewhere.

No single page was inaccurate. The site's link structure was still making an argument that its current framing didn't fully support anymore.

What this suggests, past this one case

If you're building something that doesn't fit a category that already exists at scale (a new methodology, a new kind of service, a genuinely different way of framing a familiar problem), explaining it clearly once is necessary but not sufficient. The parts of a site that aren't the explanation are also making an argument: how sections are ordered, which words appear even in the course of rejecting them, which pages point to which. If that argument still leans toward the old category, a model reading the page may weigh the structure over the sentence that says otherwise.

This isn't a claim that AI reads poorly. It's a claim about what happens at the edge of a category with very little data behind it: the gap tends to get filled from the nearest well-populated neighborhood, not left empty. That's a reasonable default for a system working from limited signal. It just means the burden sits with the site to make that signal less limited: structurally, not only declaratively.

IC Eight rebuilt its own site with this in mind: the diagnostic claim was strengthened at the top of the page, the Framework was framed explicitly as a specialized methodology rather than left to compete with the primary positioning, and the internal links that had been quietly reinforcing it as central were redirected toward pages that better reflect where it actually sits today.

Whether that fully resolves how AI systems currently describe the company is still an open question. Some of it depends on when those systems next revisit the pages that changed, which, as it turns out, is its own kind of question a company doesn't get to fully control.
