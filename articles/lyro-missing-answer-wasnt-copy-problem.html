Lyro: The Missing Answer Wasn't a Copy Problem.

Product Value Evaluation #021 · Published September 2026

Lyro turned a real specialist website into a useful customer-facing agent surprisingly quickly.

Using the public IC Eight site as its knowledge source, it answered most of a fixed set of nuanced Japanese questions accurately and conversationally. It distinguished Product Value Evaluation from Product Value Diagnosis, declined to promise unsupported business outcomes, acknowledged when pricing rationale was not available, linked back to source pages, and suggested human contact when the available evidence was insufficient.

The most revealing result, however, was one of the questions it could not answer completely.

Asked whether Product Value Evaluations were effectively AI-only, Lyro did not explain the human judgment built into the process, even though the website already contained explicit language about it.

At first, that looked like a content problem.

It was not that simple.

The later audit showed that the relevant PVE hub page had never been included in Lyro's six-page imported source set.

That changed the interpretation of the failure, and ultimately made Lyro more useful than a simple support bot.

Evaluation Setup

The evaluation used one simulated first-use scenario: a small specialist consultancy that wants visitors to understand a relatively nuanced service model without forcing them to navigate several pages before asking a question.

The real public IC Eight website was used as the knowledge source. The Tidio interface was used in English, while the fixed first-use questions were asked in Japanese. Testing took place physically in Brazil and concerned a Japan-facing business.

The first-use task was deliberately narrow: configure Lyro from the existing website and ask nine questions covering service differences, scope, pricing, unsupported outcome claims, AI involvement, and what a visitor should do when the answer is not available.

The purpose was not to test a public deployment or measure support deflection. It was to answer a more basic question:

Can Lyro turn an existing specialist website into a useful customer-facing agent without requiring the operator to rewrite the site into chatbot-specific content first?

After first use was frozen, a separate persona-free audit examined source coverage, manual repair, website-side repair, language configuration, quota behavior, handoff controls, and the broader product surface.

This was one simulated first-use lens, not recruited-user research.

Key Findings

  • Lyro produced strong first-use value from the existing website. It handled most of nine nuanced Japanese questions accurately, resisted unsupported claims, linked to source pages, and suggested human contact when evidence was insufficient.
  • Its most important miss was primarily a source-coverage problem, not proof that the website copy itself was inadequate. The PVE hub containing explicit human-judgment language was absent from the six imported website pages.
  • One manual Q&A repaired both the exact question and a related misleading premise. That demonstrated useful operator control rather than complete initial coverage.
  • A stronger repair happened on the website itself. After an explicit public FAQ answer was added and the updated FAQ page was re-imported, the same misconception was answered correctly without the manual Lyro Q&A enabled.
  • Language matching was a separate deployment condition. Correct retrieval still produced English answers to Japanese questions until Japanese support was enabled. Under “All,” Lyro answered Japanese in Japanese and Portuguese in Portuguese from the same English FAQ source.
  • Lyro became useful as a website-audit probe. Weak answers could be preserved, source coverage inspected, one variable changed, and the result retested in Playground without consuming the live conversation allowance.
  • The evaluator judged that Lyro's human-contact behavior could reduce navigation friction. When Lyro could not fully answer, it surfaced a direct contact path instead of requiring the user to find and complete a separate contact journey. No live visitor handoff was tested.

First Use Was Stronger Than the Onboarding Suggested

The onboarding was long.

The supplied setup sequence contained at least twelve questions about company type, role, support structure, business goals, and other profile information before the product reached its core value.

Once Playground opened, however, the product became much clearer.

Lyro had ingested six pages from the IC Eight website and generated site-specific prompts rather than behaving like an empty general-purpose chatbot.

The nine-question test covered distinctions that are easy for a generic FAQ layer to flatten.

Could it explain the difference between Product Value Evaluation and Product Value Diagnosis?

Could it tell a prospective client whether IC Eight guaranteed sales results?

Could it explain why a price was set at a particular level when that rationale was not on the website?

Could it distinguish what AI does from what a human evaluator does?

Could it avoid inventing an answer and point the visitor toward a human instead?

Most of those questions went well.

Lyro synthesized information across the imported site, handled uncertainty politely, and did not convert a lack of evidence into a fabricated claim.

That made the agent feel less like a scripted FAQ widget and more like a conversational layer over the website.

The Missing Answer Wasn't Where It First Appeared to Be

The most important miss concerned the role of AI in Product Value Evaluations.

The website already contained language stating that human judgment remains central to the method.

Lyro nevertheless failed to explain that distinction completely.

The obvious interpretation would have been that the website had not explained the point clearly enough.

The later audit showed why that conclusion would have been premature.

The relevant PVE hub page was not present in Lyro's six-page imported website-source inventory.

The answer therefore had not necessarily failed because the copy was weak.

The strongest source had never entered the knowledge set.

This produced the most useful methodological lesson in the evaluation:

A missing answer is not automatically a copy diagnosis.

Several different failures can produce the same visible symptom:

the content may not exist; the content may exist but be ambiguous; the relevant page may not have been ingested; retrieval may select the wrong source; or generation may fail to synthesize the available evidence.

Those are different problems and require different repairs.

The source omission occurred far below the documented priority-scan ceiling of 60 pages, but the reason for that selection was not established. The evaluation does not show that navigation depth caused it or that the scanner behaves this way systematically.

It does show that operators need to inspect what the agent actually knows.

One Q&A Fixed the Bot. The Better Repair Improved the Website.

The first correction was made inside Lyro.

A manual Q&A explicitly answered the automation question.

That repaired the exact question and also generalized to a related misleading premise, showing that the correction was not limited to one exact string match.

That was useful.

But it also raised a more interesting question.

If the misunderstanding was important enough to correct in the chatbot, should the website itself state the answer more directly?

The evaluator decided yes.

The following FAQ entry was added publicly:

Are Product Value Evaluations automated?

No. AI supports public-evidence research, controlled product testing, and analysis. A human defines the test conditions, observes how the product is used, decides when additional evidence is needed, and reviews every conclusion before delivery or publication. AI is part of the method; it is not the final judge.

This was not a conclusion Lyro autonomously discovered.

It was a human website decision prompted by observing where the existing explanation could be made more explicit.

Because the existing FAQ source could not simply be re-synced in the observed interface, the old source was deleted with explicit approval and the updated FAQ was added using Scan single page.

The earlier manual PVE Q&A remained stored but was disabled for Lyro during the retest.

The exact automation question and a neighboring misconception were then answered correctly from the updated public FAQ alone.

That was the more valuable repair.

The bot had not merely been patched.

The website had become clearer.

Lyro Became a Website-Audit Probe

This was the point where the product became personally more interesting than expected.

A customer-support agent can also expose whether a company has actually explained itself well enough.

The practical workflow is simple: give Lyro the pages that matter, ask realistic questions a visitor might actually ask, preserve weak or awkward answers, inspect whether the relevant source was ingested, change one thing, and retest.

Playground made this especially practical because the testing observed here did not consume the account's 50 live Lyro conversations.

That makes it possible to use the agent before deployment as a structured probe of the website's own information architecture and explanation quality.

But the diagnostic value depends on human classification.

If the answer is poor, the operator still has to ask whether the right page was imported, whether the page states the answer explicitly enough, whether retrieval selected it, and whether the model synthesized it correctly.

That human layer is what prevents the website from being “fixed” for the wrong reason.

For a small company, this may be one of Lyro's more interesting secondary uses: not merely automating answers, but revealing where the public explanation becomes fragile when converted into questions.

Language Matching Was a Separate Condition

Correct content did not automatically produce the right language.

With English-only support configured, Japanese questions could retrieve the correct underlying information but still receive English answers.

Once Japanese support was enabled, the mismatch was corrected.

The final “All” configuration was then tested with Japanese and Portuguese inside one conversation.

Lyro answered Japanese in Japanese and Portuguese in Portuguese from the same English FAQ source, and the source-link label was localized as well.

That result is promising but narrow.

Only those two languages and this topic were tested. It does not establish universal multilingual reliability.

What it does establish is that knowledge correctness and language behavior should be treated separately during deployment.

A support agent can know the answer and still present it in the wrong language.

Human Contact Was Part of the Value

Another small but meaningful strength appeared when Lyro could not fully answer.

Instead of merely stopping, it could surface a direct human-contact path inside the conversation.

The evaluator judged that this could reduce a common layer of website friction.

A conventional journey may require someone to leave the current context, find Contact, open another page, inspect a form, and enter information before reaching a person.

Here, the path to human help appeared at the moment the AI reached its limit.

That felt useful in the evaluation.

The result should still be bounded carefully: handoff controls were visible, but no public widget, real visitor transfer, offline handoff experience, or live channel was tested.

The observed result therefore supports a judgment about likely navigation friction, not verified production handoff performance.

Why Lyro Rather Than a Generic Chatbot?

The clearest differentiation was not that Lyro could answer FAQ-style questions.

A general-purpose AI can do that.

The stronger product-specific value came from the integrated operator workflow: website scraping, persistent page-level sources, semantic conversational answers, source links, manual Q&A repair, single-page source addition, language controls, a no-quota Playground, human-contact controls, Suggestions, and eventual channel delivery.

That combination turns maintained website content into an inspectable customer-facing knowledge system.

A generic assistant can discuss a block of pasted copy.

Lyro provided surfaces for asking what had been ingested, correcting one knowledge point, changing supported languages, testing repeatedly, and eventually deploying the same system into customer channels.

That operational continuity is the clearer reason to use a dedicated product.

Free Access and Pricing

The account showed an initial allowance of 50 live Lyro conversations.

Playground testing did not consume that allowance.

Those first 50 conversations were described as a lifetime allowance, not a renewable monthly free tier.

On September 8, 2026, standalone Lyro with 50 renewable monthly conversations was displayed at USD 29 per month or USD 290 per year.

Starter also displayed 50 Lyro conversations, but those were labeled one-off rather than monthly renewable usage.

This evaluation does not establish whether paid adoption is economically justified for the tested site.

It does establish that the unpaid Playground experience was sufficient to discover the core product value and test the knowledge system extensively before consuming live conversations.

Broader Product Surface

The audit also exposed features beyond the first-use task, including Guidance, handoff settings, Suggestions, proactive roles, procedures, API actions, channels, and paid analytics.

None of those were outcome-tested. No widget was installed on the public site, no email channel was connected, and no real visitor conversation occurred.

The broader surface therefore supports a claim about product scope, not demonstrated production performance.

Evaluation Limits

This evaluation used one simulated persona, one real specialist website, one account, an English product interface, and a fixed set of Japanese first-use questions. It did not test recruited Japanese users, public widget behavior, live visitor conversations, email, production retrieval, repeat-run reliability, long-term source freshness, live handoff, Suggestions quality, analytics usefulness, procedures, proactive roles, API actions, or external integrations.

The website-side FAQ repair was made by a human. Lyro did not autonomously diagnose or edit the public site. The omitted PVE page was confirmed absent from the six imported pages, but the reason it was omitted was not established. Japanese and Portuguese language matching were tested only in the observed cases and should not be generalized to all supported languages or topics.

Conclusion

Lyro succeeded at the core first-use job.

It turned a real specialist website into a courteous, useful Japanese-speaking agent that could explain nuanced services, resist unsupported claims, cite sources, and direct visitors toward human help when necessary.

Its most useful failure also exposed an important deployment condition.

The missing answer initially looked like a content problem. The audit showed that the relevant page had never entered the knowledge set. A manual Q&A repaired the agent, while a later FAQ change made the website itself clearer and allowed the same questions to pass without the manual Lyro exception.

The central result is therefore broader than support automation:

Lyro can turn maintained website content into a useful customer-facing knowledge system, while also giving the operator a practical way to test whether that content is sufficiently explicit, sufficiently covered, and sufficiently retrievable.

Trustworthy performance still depends on knowing what the system has actually ingested, testing it with realistic questions, configuring language behavior correctly, and keeping a human in the diagnostic loop.

For this evaluation, the product became more valuable when it stopped being only a support bot.

It became a way to ask a second question:

Is the website itself clear enough?

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
