Tally: When AI Generation Is Only the Beginning

Product Value Evaluation #028 · Published September 2026

Tally presents itself as “the simplest way to create forms.” Its public proposition extends well beyond basic form building, covering document-like editing, conditional logic, calculations, Thank You pages, integrations, embeds, and AI-assisted creation.

The question for this Product Value Evaluation was therefore broader than whether AI could write a useful set of questions. The test asked whether a plain-language business requirement could become a working intake process with structured logic, a usable respondent experience, tested submissions, and downstream routing.

The result was a weak success.

Tally converted a detailed consultation-intake requirement into an editable conditional form. After repair, that form could be published, tested across different branches, used to collect structured submissions, trigger an owner notification, and feed both earlier and new responses into Google Sheets.

But the AI-generated form could not safely be accepted as finished. Anonymous generation produced no visible result. Signed-in generation created a substantial and useful starting point, but material defects remained. Repeated AI revisions improved the form while continuing to claim completion before its structure fully matched the requirement. A publishable result ultimately required direct inspection and manual logic repair.

This changed where Tally's value appeared. AI generation helped start the workflow, but the stronger value came from the environment that allowed the generated structure to be inspected, corrected, executed, published, and connected to the rest of the business process.

Key Findings

  • Anonymous AI generation failed silently in this session. Despite a “No signup required” entry claim, two signed-out attempts produced no form, visible error, or explicit account gate. The reason remains unknown.
  • Tally AI's completion statements did not reliably verify the generated structure. Material wording, required-state, option, and conditional-logic defects remained after the AI had reported successful completion.
  • The final conditional form required manual block-level logic repair. AI-assisted revisions improved the form substantially, but direct editing was still required before all tested branches behaved correctly.
  • The repaired form became a genuine operating workflow. Publishing, structured submissions, owner notification, Google Sheets backfill, and subsequent live synchronization all worked in the tested sequence.
  • Tally's strongest demonstrated differentiation was the environment around generation. Editable structure, executable logic, Preview, hosted respondents, response management, notification, and downstream routing mattered more than form-copy generation alone.
  • The overall result was a weak success. The intended workflow was ultimately achieved, but not with the low-friction reliability that would justify treating the first AI result as finished.
  • Japanese support was visible but narrowly demonstrated. Japanese could be selected for Tally-supplied respondent interface text, while Japanese custom copy and AI-generation quality were not tested.

The First AI Result Was Useful, but Not Dependable

The first-use path began without an account. Tally prominently offered “Create a free form” and “No signup required,” and its AI creation option opened a blank editor.

The same frozen prompt was submitted twice. Neither attempt produced a form, a visible error, or an explicit request to create an account. The interface simply returned to an empty prompt state.

Signup was therefore chosen as a recovery step. It was not demonstrated to be a technical requirement for AI generation.

Once signed in, the identical prompt generated a substantial result. Tally reported 42 blocks and five logic rules. This was already more valuable than receiving a prose outline from a general-purpose AI: the output existed as editable form structure with conditional behavior.

The problem emerged during inspection.

The first form contained useful structure and working branches, but it also diverged from the fixed requirement in wording, answer options, required states, and conditional behavior. A larger AI revision made extensive changes and then reported that the form was fully aligned with the specification. Direct inspection showed that important mismatches remained.

Several narrower correction prompts improved the form further, but the same pattern continued. Tally AI could make useful structural changes while overestimating how completely those changes had satisfied the request.

That distinction is important because the AI was not producing an unstructured artifact. Tally itself knew the actual block structure, field types, logic rules, and required states. Its natural-language completion statement could therefore be compared with the artifact it had just modified.

In this session, those two sources did not always agree.

Conditional Logic Exposed the Verification Gap

The most consequential remaining problem involved conditional visibility.

The form was intended to qualify consultation inquiries according to factors such as product stage, Japan-market status, evidence, timing, and budget readiness. Conditional fields therefore affected the information the operator would receive, not merely how the form looked.

Some generated branches worked, but complete visibility for grouped fields eventually required manual editing in the logic editor. The relevant field title and its child option blocks had to be targeted together. Repeated AI-assisted revisions had not resolved that structural detail.

After manual repair, four freshly loaded Preview cases passed the required Japan and budget paths.

Fresh loads were important because changing answers repeatedly within one Preview could leave an earlier branch visible until the form was reloaded. That observation does not establish a broad runtime defect, but it reinforced the need to test the actual respondent logic rather than infer correctness from the editor or AI completion message.

This is where Tally's dedicated environment began to justify itself more clearly.

The generated form could be inspected as executable structure. The logic could be opened and repaired directly. Preview made it possible to test different respondent paths. Once the issue was understood, the user did not need to leave Tally and reconstruct the form elsewhere.

AI generation had created the scaffold. Tally's form system made verification and repair possible.

The Stronger Value Appeared After Generation

Once the structure was repaired, the workflow became much more convincing.

The form was published on Tally's hosted respondent surface. Two approved synthetic responses completed successfully and appeared as intelligible, branch-specific submission rows.

An owner email notification was enabled and later triggered as expected on a new submission.

A dedicated Google Sheet was connected. The two submissions that already existed were backfilled into the Sheet, and a later response appeared as a new row through live synchronization.

At that point, the result was no longer simply an AI-generated form.

It had become a small operating system for the intake process: editable structure, executable conditional logic, hosted respondent experience, publication, structured responses, owner awareness, and spreadsheet routing existed inside one environment.

This also clarifies Tally's incremental value relative to other tools.

A general-purpose AI can draft questions, describe conditional logic, or generate code for a form. A minimal endpoint service can receive form submissions with relatively little infrastructure. Tally's demonstrated advantage in this evaluation was the combination of those pieces into an editable no-code workflow.

The important comparison was therefore not whether Tally AI could write better questions.

It was whether the dedicated product could carry the result further than question generation.

In this test, it could.

Generation and Verification Should Not Be the Same Claim

The main weakness was not that Tally AI required revision. Complex generated artifacts commonly do.

The more product-specific problem was that Tally had enough structural knowledge to make stronger claims about whether the requested artifact was actually complete, but its AI completion language did not consistently reflect that state.

This creates an opportunity that a general-purpose AI does not have.

Tally can inspect its own form graph. It knows which blocks exist, which fields are required, which options belong to which questions, which conditional rules are active, and how the form behaves in Preview.

Instead of treating “changes applied” and “request verified” as equivalent, the product could make that distinction explicit.

For a complex generated form, Tally could show a checkable summary of requested fields, exact options, required states, and branches after AI generation or revision. Conditional logic could be tested automatically against fresh-state cases derived from the current rule graph. A field whose title becomes visible while its child options remain hidden could be flagged before the AI reports that the requested change is complete.

These are not generic suggestions to improve AI accuracy. They arise from the structure Tally already owns.

The same principle applies to the anonymous generation failure. The cause remains unknown, but Tally is the product capable of telling the user whether generation is waiting on account state, quota, retry, or another condition. Returning silently to an empty prompt made the problem harder to interpret than it needed to be.

The Business Value Depends on the Full Operating Loop

The simulated persona in this evaluation was a solo operator at a small specialist consultancy, without a dedicated sales or operations team and without developer skills.

For that user, a visually polished form with incorrect logic would not be a partial business success. It could make qualification less reliable by collecting the wrong information or presenting the wrong branch to a prospect.

The value appeared only when the complete process worked.

This is why the final result qualifies as a weak success rather than either a strong success or a failure.

The AI did meaningful work. It substantially reduced the effort required to reach an editable conditional form. But the user still had to inspect the form, issue multiple correction requests, recognize that AI completion claims were not verification, and repair part of the logic manually.

Once that work was done, Tally delivered something substantially more useful than generated form copy.

It delivered an operating workflow.

Implications for Tally

The clearest product opportunity is to strengthen the transition from generated scaffold to verified workflow.

Tally could make structural verification part of the AI creation experience, particularly for forms with conditional logic. A checkable specification view, automated branch testing, clearer distinction between applied changes and verified requirements, and warnings for incomplete conditional field groups would address problems that appeared directly in this evaluation.

The operational value could also be surfaced earlier. Once a form becomes structurally valid, Tally could guide a first-time user through a short sequence covering publication, a test submission, owner notification, and a downstream destination. Those later stages were where the product's dedicated environment demonstrated its strongest value.

The first generation was useful. The completed operating loop was more useful.

The gap between them is where the product has the most to gain.

Evaluation Limits

This evaluation used one approved simulated persona rather than a recruited external participant. One complex B2B intake specification and one account were tested on one day in Chrome on a Mac physically located in Brazil.

The nominal first-use time limit was treated as a patience checkpoint rather than a hard cutoff. The session continued because the user explicitly chose to complete the still-coherent workflow, so the total duration should not be interpreted as ordinary effortless signup-to-value time.

The cause of the anonymous AI non-result remains unknown. The experiment does not establish that signup is technically required or indicate a general failure rate for Tally AI.

Four Preview paths and three synthetic live submissions were exercised. Owner notification was demonstrated once after enablement. Google Sheets was tested with two backfilled responses and one subsequent live synchronization. Long-term reliability, retries, schema changes, and other integration behavior were not evaluated.

The test form remained in English. Japanese was observed only as an option for built-in respondent interface text; Japanese AI generation, custom-copy quality, typography, layout, and Japanese-language submissions remain untested.

Other visible integrations and capabilities were not treated as demonstrated behavior. The exact account plan label was not separately verified, no paid upgrade was completed, and the tested core workflow did not encounter a payment gate.

Conclusion

Within those limits, the result is clear: Tally AI substantially accelerated the creation of a conditional form, but generation was only the beginning.

The stronger product value appeared when that scaffold became something the user could inspect, repair, verify, publish, operate, and connect to the rest of the business workflow.

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
