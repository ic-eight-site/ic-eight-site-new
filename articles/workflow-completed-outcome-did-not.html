Diagnosis & Priority

The Workflow Completed. The Outcome Did Not.

Published August 2026

AI product evaluation often stops too early.

A background was removed. A new scene was generated. A shadow was added. A file was exported. Each operation completed successfully, so the product appears to have worked.

But the user may still not have the result they wanted.

That distinction became clear during IC Eight's evaluation of Photoroom. The experiment began with a simple goal: turn an ordinary product photo into an image that could realistically be used for online selling. Photoroom completed several individual operations successfully. Yet an error introduced early in the process remained visible in the final result. Later steps added polish around that error rather than correcting it.

The workflow completed. The outcome did not.

This suggests a useful way to evaluate AI products beyond individual feature performance.

Operation-First and Outcome-First Products

Some products are organized around operations.

The user decides what needs to happen, in what order, and which tool should be used next: remove the background, replace it, add a shadow, adjust the crop, choose a template, export the result.

The AI performs each selected operation.

Other products are organized more directly around outcomes.

The user describes the result they want: create a product image suitable for an online store, turn a long video into a publishable social clip, or create a presentation for a management meeting. The product takes greater responsibility for deciding which intermediate steps are necessary.

Neither model is inherently better. Experienced users may prefer direct control over individual operations. But when a product asks users to design the workflow themselves, it may automate execution without reducing the judgment required to complete the task.

AI can therefore remove work at one level while creating work at another.

Feature Success Is Not Task Success

This distinction also changes what counts as success.

A successful operation means that a specific function produced an output.

A successful task means that the user can actually use the result for the intended purpose.

For an ecommerce image, the question is not whether background removal succeeded. It is whether the final image is suitable for selling the product.

For an AI video tool, generating a video file is not enough if the result still requires substantial repair before publication.

For an analysis product, producing charts and explanations is not the same as giving the user something reliable enough to support a decision.

A successful operation is not necessarily a successful task.

What Happens to Upstream Errors?

Multi-step AI products introduce another important question: what happens when an early stage is wrong?

In the Photoroom experiment, later operations continued normally after an imperfect product extraction. A background was generated. A shadow was added. The image increasingly resembled a finished commercial asset.

But the original error remained.

This creates a particular kind of failure. The output gains visual completeness without gaining underlying correctness.

If later stages do not inspect or repair earlier mistakes, the product may not be coordinating a task as a whole. It may simply be passing the output of one operation into the next.

That distinction becomes increasingly important as AI products combine more capabilities into apparently unified workflows.

Three Questions for Evaluating AI Workflows

Future Product Value Evaluations will use three additional questions when examining multi-step AI products:

Who designs the path to the outcome: the user or the product?

Did individual features succeed, or did the user's actual task succeed?

Can later stages detect and repair earlier failures, or do they simply make those failures look more finished?

These questions help reveal something feature lists rarely show: how much of the user's work the product has actually taken responsibility for.

An AI product can complete every step it was asked to perform and still leave the user with an unfinished job.

That difference is worth measuring.

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
