Lovable: The App Worked. The Research Still Needed an Auditor.

Product Value Evaluation #020 · Published September 2026

Lovable promises something broader than page generation: describe software in ordinary language, receive a functional application, and keep refining it conversationally without first deciding on a technical architecture.

In this evaluation, that promise was convincing.

A non-technical founder described a specialized prospect-research workflow for a small B2B consultancy. Lovable turned the brief into an interactive application with candidate cards, evidence links, Approve/Reject controls, and a never-send outreach-draft path.

The user did not choose a framework, database design, or application architecture. No code was edited manually.

But the application's apparent completeness raised a harder question.

Could the research inside it be trusted?

That remained unresolved.

Evaluation Setup

The fixed first-use scenario represented the founder of a small specialist B2B consultancy with no sales or engineering team.

The task was to build a prospect-research application for identifying technology companies with credible public evidence of a gap between intended product value and what users actually discover, particularly in Japan.

A Japan launch, Japanese website, localization activity, category fit, or general market statistics were explicitly insufficient. The app needed concrete same-company evidence, source links, a grounded contact or role, Approve/Reject controls, and a draft workflow that would never send outreach automatically.

The evaluation therefore tested two different capabilities at once:

Can Lovable turn a specialized business process into working software?

And:

Can the resulting application perform the underlying research with enough evidence discipline to support action?

The formal first-use evaluation was frozen before later hardening work began. A separate persona-free audit then tested evidence quality, persistence, freshness, retrieval behavior, and provenance.

Key Findings

  • Lovable demonstrated strong application-building value. A specialized business requirement became an interactive bespoke application without the user making architecture or coding decisions.
  • The first complete-looking prospect result was more confident than its evidence justified. Independent audit found candidates supported by historical problems, broad market statistics, or evidence that did not satisfy the app's own qualification rule.
  • Natural-language correction materially changed application behavior. Lovable tightened admission logic, later added persistence, freshness gates, diagnostics, and retrieval controls without requiring manual code changes.
  • The main bottleneck shifted from business logic to evidence retrieval. Once stricter rules were enforced, the app sometimes returned zero candidates because too few source pages could be read reliably.
  • A provider-linked URL correction materially improved retrieval, from 3 readable pages out of 29 attempts to 24 out of 36. But the final run did not preserve enough candidate-level evidence to establish that the resulting shortlist was trustworthy.
  • The strongest demonstrated workflow was therefore human-guided system building, not autonomous prospect qualification.

Lovable Built the Workflow Before the User Had to Think About Architecture

The first-use value appeared quickly.

Lovable accepted a detailed business requirement and began creating the application without asking the user to choose a stack, database, framework, or application structure.

The first build did encounter a parser failure. Lovable exposed a visible repair path and fixed it without requiring technical diagnosis from the user.

Once functional, the application looked surprisingly complete.

It contained prospect cards, reasons for selection, public signals, quotations, source links, contact information, Approve/Reject controls, and an outreach-draft workflow.

The first search returned five candidates.

At that point, Lovable had already demonstrated something meaningfully different from a general-purpose AI conversation.

The business process had become software.

The problem was that the software looked more finished than the research inside it actually was.

The App Was Stronger Than the Evidence

Some of the underlying sources were real.

That was not enough.

Independent audit showed that some of those sources did not answer the question the application claimed they answered.

One candidate was supported by a historical product problem that had already been remedied. Another relied on broad Japanese AI-adoption data rather than a company-specific product-value problem. A third had a genuine regional product limitation, but no verified buying signal.

This exposed a central distinction:

Evidence can be real without being relevant enough to justify the conclusion attached to it.

The application had successfully created the structure of a disciplined research workflow.

It had not yet demonstrated disciplined research judgment.

A natural-language correction then tightened the rule: require a concrete same-company problem signal, separate observation from inference, reject generic Japan or market evidence, place sources beside material claims, and avoid invented contacts.

The rerun returned three candidates instead of padding the list back to five.

That restraint was encouraging.

But the first-use task had asked for five dependable prospects.

The corrected application had not produced them.

One Correction Improved the Logic. The Audit Kept Finding New Failure Modes.

The later hardening work became less about interface design and more about progressively discovering what “trustworthy research” actually required.

Persistence was one example.

The first-use preview lost shortlist, decision, and draft state after refresh.

Later testing showed that browser-local persistence could work under a stable origin, and that the apparent state loss was partly caused by preview-origin changes.

That improved the interpretation of the original failure, but it also showed why users could not infer reliability from the first experience alone.

Freshness created another layer.

Once the app was required to use recent evidence rather than stale material, one strict run returned zero candidates instead of lowering the standard.

That was a positive behavior.

It also revealed the next bottleneck: the application could not reliably retrieve enough usable source pages to evaluate prospects under its stronger evidence contract.

At the auditor's request, Lovable then instrumented the retrieval pipeline.

A diagnostic attempted 29 URLs.

Only three were readable.

Nineteen were not found, six were blocked or forbidden, and one timed out.

At that point, the problem was no longer vaguely “AI research quality.”

A concrete upstream failure had become visible.

Retrieval Improved Dramatically Once URL Provenance Improved

The final hardening step constrained enumeration to provider-linked URLs rather than allowing weaker URL construction.

The resulting change was substantial.

Readable yield improved from:

3 / 29 = 10.3%

to:

24 / 36 = 66.7%

while not-found failures dropped from 19 to 3.

That is one of the strongest results in the evaluation.

A non-technical user had not designed a retrieval architecture.

The audit identified a failure in terms of product behavior.

Lovable translated that requirement into changed software.

This is exactly where the product's application-building value remained strongest.

But the improvement did not establish that autonomous prospect research now worked.

The final run reported five qualifying evidence items and three admitted candidates, yet the full URL ledger, two candidate identities, and most candidate-level evidence were not preserved.

The aggregate retrieval numbers could be inspected.

The final business conclusions could not.

So the strongest defensible conclusion is narrower:

Lovable materially improved the evidence pipeline. It did not establish that the resulting prospect decisions were trustworthy.

The Auditor Was Part of the Successful Workflow

This evaluation would likely have reached a very different conclusion without the persona-free audit.

A normal user could easily have stopped at the first five candidate cards.

The application looked structured.

Some sources were real.

The system reported successful activity.

Later results looked stricter.

None of those surfaces independently revealed the full trust problem.

The audit had to distinguish current evidence from historical evidence, company-specific product problems from broad market statistics, relevant sources from merely real sources, and successful URL retrieval from unsupported prospect admission.

That changes the product interpretation.

The strongest demonstrated workflow was not:

Lovable autonomously discovers trustworthy prospects.

It was:

Lovable builds and evolves the research system while a skilled human defines and tests the evidence contract.

That is still substantial value.

It is simply not the same value.

Why Lovable Rather Than a General-Purpose AI?

A general-purpose AI can search for companies, summarize sources, propose qualification rules, and draft outreach.

Lovable's clearer differentiation was that those requirements became an operable application.

The user could move from research idea, to custom workflow, to candidate state, to Approve/Reject, to draft, to changed business rules, to changed retrieval architecture, without translating those decisions into code.

The most important capability was therefore not autonomous research intelligence.

It was the ability to turn increasingly precise business judgment into increasingly precise software behavior.

The user's understanding of the problem improved during the evaluation.

Lovable allowed the application to improve with it.

That is a stronger product-specific case than “AI can build a prospecting app in one prompt.”

Free Access Was Enough to Build, but Not Always Enough to Verify

The Free plan was sufficient to demonstrate the core application-building workflow.

But later hardening repeatedly encountered the daily allowance before implementation and decisive verification could always be completed in one session.

That is one reason the audit stretched across multiple days.

This report does not treat that cadence as universal Lovable development speed, nor does it infer paid-plan performance from the Free account.

The relevant observation is narrower: Free access exposed enough value to build and materially improve the application, but the stop-start allowance made rigorous iterative verification slower.

Evaluation Limits

This evaluation used one simulated persona, one specialist consultancy workflow, one Free account, an English interface, and a business problem focused on Japan.

It was not recruited Japanese-user research.

The first-use verdict remained frozen even though later audit work substantially improved the prototype.

The final reported figures of 36 attempted URLs, 24 readable pages, five qualifying evidence items, and three admitted candidates came from one later browser run and were not independently reproduced.

The final three candidates were not independently validated, and complete final provenance was not retained.

Production deployment, backend persistence, external connectors, Git workflows, published-app behavior, multi-user collaboration, longer-term reliability, repeat-run variance, paid-plan development cadence, and exact per-run economics were not established.

No outreach was sent.

Conclusion

Lovable's strongest achievement in this evaluation was not autonomous prospecting.

It was software construction.

A non-technical founder described an unusually specific business workflow and received an operable application.

When the business rule proved too weak, the rule could be changed in ordinary language.

When persistence, freshness, retrieval, and URL provenance later failed, those problems could also be translated into changed application behavior without requiring the user to become a software engineer.

That is substantial product-specific value.

But the research output repeatedly looked more trustworthy than it was.

The first shortlist failed independent evidence checks. Later hardening materially improved retrieval, yet the final candidate-level evidence was not preserved well enough to verify the resulting shortlist.

The distinction is therefore simple:

Lovable demonstrated that it could build and evolve the prospecting software. It did not demonstrate that the software could autonomously decide which prospects were trustworthy.

For a specialized business user, that still opens an important possibility.

The user does not have to know how to build the system.

But someone still has to know what evidence should count.

In this evaluation, that judgment remained human.

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
