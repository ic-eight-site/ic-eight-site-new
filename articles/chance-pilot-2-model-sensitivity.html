AI Can Agree on What to Reject and Still Disagree on What to Do Next

Chance Pilot #2 · A Product Value Discovery experiment · Published August 2026

Four AI models were given the same evidence about the same product and asked the same strategic question.

They didn't agree on what the product should become. But they agreed, more than expected, on what it should stop relying on.

That asymmetry is the finding worth sitting with.

The setup

The experiment used Chance AI, a visual-AI consumer app, as a fixed case. Four models were given an identical Neutral Evidence Packet — first-use persona sessions, a capability audit, and documented reliability issues — and asked to identify the strongest commercial opportunities.

Three of the models ran the comparison directly: GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Sonnet 5, and Gemini 3.6 Flash each received the same packet and the same prompt in a fresh context, with no access to web search and no visibility into the other models' answers. A fourth, Codex running on GPT-5.6 Sol Medium, was added as a further comparison point. Codex had already operated Chance directly during an earlier phase of product audit and had produced its own strategy judgment before this comparison began; that answer was folded in afterward rather than generated under identical conditions.

The persona evidence in the packet came from structured role-play, not recruited-user research. That distinction matters for how much weight any single observation should carry, and it's worth remembering as you read the summary below.

Where the models agreed

All four moved away from treating generic single-image recognition — point a camera at something, get an explanation — as Chance's strongest commercial asset.

The reasoning differed by model, but the direction didn't. GPT called the long-explanation format commercially overrated. Claude called basic visual search the most substitutable capability in the product. Gemini kept visual analysis only as part of a broader bundle rather than as the lead offer. Codex located the value in repeatable workflows rather than in the ability to talk about a single photo.

None of the four ignored the documented reliability problems either — a misidentified painting, a misclassified shoe, a shopping match that was off by an order of magnitude. Each treated those failures as a real constraint on what could be built on top of the recognition layer, not as noise to route around.

That's two points of convergence: the front door isn't the asset, and known failures narrow what's buildable. Under one evidence base and one prompt, both held across models with genuinely different training and tuning. That's a reasonable signal that these are closer to structural constraints than stylistic opinions.

Where they split

Once the models moved past rejecting the current core, they stopped agreeing.

GPT proposed a B2B visual research workspace, built around accumulating visual context over time for design and merchandising teams. Claude proposed a persistent visual life-journal, built around personal memory and archive value for individual subscribers. Gemini proposed an art-and-design education product, built around guided observation and vocabulary. Codex proposed operational and campaign infrastructure, built around repeatable photo-to-output workflows for brands.

The specifics of any one proposal aren't the point. What matters is the shape of the split: four models looking at identical evidence identified four different hidden assets, four different reasons a user would come back, four different buyers, and four different business models. The disagreement didn't start at the final recommendation. It started one layer earlier, at the question of which underused feature actually carried the latent value — and everything downstream followed from that earlier, quieter choice.

Two different kinds of signal

That gives a working distinction that seems more useful than "the models disagreed, so AI strategy is unreliable" or "the models agreed on X, so X must be correct."

Consensus can expose constraints. When independent models, working from the same evidence, converge on rejecting an assumption, that's worth treating as a signal about the evidence itself — not proof, but a reasonable prior that the constraint is real rather than a single model's house style.

Divergence can expose decision frontiers. When models diverge, that's not a failure to resolve. It's a map of where a real strategic choice still has to be made by someone accountable for the outcome. The four proposals in this pilot aren't four candidates to rank and pick from. They're four different theories of where value might live, each internally consistent, none of them validated against an actual market.

That reframes what multi-model comparison is useful for. Not as a panel that votes on the right answer — nothing here suggests the majority view, or any single model's view, is correct. As a way of mapping which parts of a strategic question are load-bearing and stable, and which parts are still genuinely open.

What this doesn't show

This was one controlled experiment, with one evidence packet, one product, and one strategic prompt. It doesn't establish that AI models generally converge on constraints and diverge on direction — only that they did, in this instance, under conditions designed to isolate model choice as the variable.

It also doesn't tell you which of the four proposed directions Chance should pursue, or whether any of them would survive contact with real users. The role-play personas in the evidence packet were a controlled substitute for user research, not a replacement for it.

What it does show is where the judgment still sits. The convergence narrows the space worth arguing about. The divergence marks where someone still has to decide — and that decision doesn't get any easier just because four models each had an opinion.
