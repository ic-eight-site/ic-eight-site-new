Product Value Discovery

What AI Discovers Depends on What You Let It Observe

A Product Value Discovery Case Study Using Chance AI · Published August 2026

AI is increasingly capable of reviewing interfaces, simulating users, analyzing positioning, and proposing product strategy. That raises an uncomfortable question for work like ours:

How much of Product Value Discovery can AI actually do?

Rather than answer that abstractly, I tested it on a real product.

The product was Chance AI, a camera-first visual AI app. Chance positions itself around the idea that users should be able to point a camera at the world and understand what they are seeing without first having to formulate a search query or prompt.

The purpose of the exercise was not to produce another app review. It was to test something narrower:

If AI is given different kinds of evidence about the same product, does it discover the same product value and the same commercial opportunities?

It did not.

What changed was not simply the quality of the answer. What changed was what the AI believed the product was.

Stage 1: Inferring the likely users from public information

I did not begin by defining a target persona myself.

AI first reviewed Chance’s public positioning, product descriptions, founder statements, and other available material, then inferred the types of users the product appeared to be targeting.

From that analysis, three Japanese first-use personas were selected for testing:

  • an ordinary university student close to the broad Gen Z target;
  • an art/design student with a strong interest in visual culture;
  • a vintage-fashion and secondhand-shopping enthusiast.

The point was not to invent ideal users for Chance. It was to approximate plausible users based on the product’s own public signals.

Each persona was then handed to Codex, which operated the actual Chance app through iPhone Mirroring.

The instruction was deliberately restrained. Codex was not told to explore every feature, demonstrate the product’s value, or prove a hypothesis. It was told to behave naturally as a first-time user and stop when that user realistically would.

That distinction mattered.

Method note

The constraints changed deliberately at each stage. During first-use testing, Codex was instructed not to explore every feature or try to prove the product useful. It was asked to behave like a plausible first-time user with limited patience.

Later, those constraints were removed and Codex was told to audit the product broadly, including capabilities a normal user might never discover.

The prompts and interaction logs were preserved throughout the experiment.

Stage 2: What first-time users actually discovered

The three simulated users did not discover the same product.

The general university student understood the basic proposition quickly: photograph something and receive an AI-generated explanation with more context than simple object recognition.

But understanding the proposition did not produce activation.

After trying the sample experience, the user had no natural desire to photograph something nearby. The product was understood, but there was no immediate next use.

The art/design student went considerably further.

Here, Chance became useful not simply because it identified objects, but because it helped turn a vague visual impression into vocabulary and things to notice. In one artwork interaction, it directed attention toward composition, texture, negative space, and visual movement.

That suggested a more interesting form of value: not answering a question the user already had, but helping the user form better questions.

However, Chance also misidentified an artwork. Worse, the incorrect attribution generated a chain of plausible contextual explanation around the wrong artist and work.

That changed the product’s value equation. A tool intended to deepen visual understanding became something the user felt required external verification.

The vintage-fashion user discovered yet another Chance.

This user chose an outfit-analysis flow. Chance provided styling commentary and suggestions, but the advice was fairly generic and some visual details were misread. More importantly, the user did not particularly want an AI stylist.

For someone already interested in fashion, the useful role was not “tell me how to dress correctly.” It was closer to “show me something about this garment that I have not noticed.”

Across all three tests, one pattern was striking:

None of the users naturally explored Chance very deeply.

They stopped when they had received enough value, or enough information to decide there was no reason to continue.

This is closer to real first-use behavior than exhaustive product exploration. People do not normally inspect every feature simply because it exists.

But that creates a problem for product analysis.

Stage 3: Asking AI to infer the business from first-use evidence

I then gave the first-use findings to a separate ChatGPT that had never used Chance and asked it to reconsider the product’s commercial direction.

The AI was explicitly allowed to change the buyer, end user, business model, distribution model, and product packaging.

The strategy shifted sharply away from Chance’s original broad consumer framing.

It proposed a range of mostly B2B opportunities, including a Visual Customer Support Agent, a Marketplace Catalog Intelligence API, a Field Service Visual Copilot, Photo-first Maintenance Intake, and embedded retail use cases. It also proposed directions such as a Cultural Venue Platform and Visual Learning.

These were commercially plausible ideas.

But they immediately raised another question:

Why Chance?

Many of the proposed businesses could also be built around ChatGPT, Gemini, Meta AI, or another general-purpose multimodal model.

A Visual Customer Support Agent might be a viable AI product. A field-service visual copilot might also have commercial value. But neither idea necessarily depended on assets that were distinctive to Chance.

There was another limitation hidden inside the exercise.

The AI could reason only from the evidence it had been given.

And the three first-use users had never discovered much of the product.

Features that did not appear in their journeys effectively did not exist in the strategy analysis.

Stage 4: Removing the persona and auditing the product itself

So I changed the task.

Codex stopped role-playing a user.

Instead, it became a product auditor and was instructed to explore Chance broadly, including features a normal first-time user might never reach.

This produced a very different picture of the product.

Chance was no longer merely a camera-based interpretation tool.

The audit found a broader system involving visual search, plant recognition, outfit analysis, photo transformations, poster-like outputs, skin and beauty experiences, visual agents, Snap history, Insights, recurring summaries, personalization, sharing, social posting, and memory-related features.

More importantly, Codex began to see these not as isolated features but as a connected product architecture:

capture → specialized agent → interpretation or transformation → shareable output → persistent Snap → accumulated patterns and summaries

That architecture had been largely invisible in the first-use tests.

This changed the strategic analysis again.

Stage 5: The strategy changed when the evidence changed

After exploring the product directly, Codex was asked the same broader commercial question:

Forget the current consumer positioning. Based on what you actually observed, where are the strongest commercial opportunities?

Its top recommendation was no longer a generic support, field-service, or visual-search workflow.

It proposed a branded visual campaign platform.

A brand could distribute a specialized visual agent that turns customer or staff photos into controlled, branded outputs: product cards, campaign visuals, event content, or shareable assets.

Another recommendation moved even closer to a seller workflow: a system for vintage stores, antique sellers, independent boutiques, and other merchants to turn product photography into attributes, descriptions, visual assets, inventory cards, and channel-specific content.

Other proposals used Snap history, recurring Insights, and visual agents for field operations, clienteling, and beauty services.

These ideas were much more tightly connected to capabilities actually present in Chance.

The important result is not whether any of these businesses is correct.

None has yet been validated by paying customers.

The important result is that the opportunity map changed when the AI gained access to a different layer of evidence.

Public information shaped which users the AI expected to matter.

First-use evidence produced one picture of the product and one set of commercial possibilities.

Direct product exploration produced another.

First-use value and embedded product potential are not the same thing

This experiment exposed a distinction that matters beyond Chance.

A first-use test answers questions such as:

  • What does a user understand?
  • What value do they actually discover?
  • Where do they stop?
  • What do they ignore?
  • What would make them come back?

Those are essential questions.

But they do not answer another question:

What commercially valuable capabilities are embedded in the product but remain undiscovered by first-time users?

Those are different forms of value.

A capability that no ordinary user discovers during first use may represent a discoverability failure.

But it may also represent a latent product asset with potential for a completely different buyer, workflow, or business model.

If strategy is inferred only from first-use behavior, that second category can disappear from the analysis.

AI can make strategic leaps, but it does not escape its evidence

One result surprised me.

The AI did not need to be told to preserve Chance’s current target, positioning, or business model.

It was willing to discard large parts of the consumer product, separate the buyer from the end user, move from B2C to B2B or B2B2C, and promote secondary capabilities over headline features.

That is substantial strategic reasoning.

But there was also a recurring weakness.

When the strategy AI worked from first-use findings without direct access to the broader product, it was more likely to fall into commercially plausible but generic categories such as customer support, field service, or workflow automation.

Those ideas may have good economics.

But they raise another question:

Why Chance?

If the proposed opportunity could just as naturally be built with ChatGPT, Gemini, Meta AI, or another multimodal model, it may be a good AI business idea without being a good use of this particular product’s assets.

Direct product exploration improved this considerably because the AI had more Chance-specific evidence to work with.

But even after the AI had explored the broader product directly, its opportunity map did not converge with the one I had developed independently from the same product.

There was some overlap in the audiences identified, but not in what the product itself should become or which capabilities should define its value.

I am not including that alternative commercial direction here. The point is not that the human answer was necessarily better. It is that broader access to the product did not cause the AI and human analyses to converge.

That matters because it suggests that evidence access is only part of the problem. What gets connected, reframed, or treated as commercially consequential can still produce different strategic paths from the same underlying product.

Product discovery is partly an observation problem

It is tempting to frame AI strategy as a reasoning problem.

Give the model enough intelligence, and it should identify the right market, buyer, positioning, and business model.

This experiment suggests something more complicated.

Before reasoning begins, someone or something has to determine:

  • which users to observe;
  • which behaviors matter;
  • how long to follow them;
  • whether to stop at naturally discovered value;
  • whether to inspect features users never reach;
  • which outputs deserve to be treated as assets;
  • which errors invalidate a use case;
  • and which capabilities are merely interesting versus commercially consequential.

The strategy is downstream of those decisions.

That may be the central finding of this experiment.

AI was capable of making substantial strategic leaps. It could change the buyer, discard the current positioning, move from B2C to B2B2C, and elevate capabilities that the product itself barely emphasized.

What it could not do was escape the evidence environment it had been given.

Change the evidence, and the product changed.

Change the product that the AI could see, and the opportunity map changed with it.

This makes Product Value Discovery less a question of whether AI can “do strategy” and more a question of how evidence is constructed before strategy begins.

What this means for Product Value Discovery

The experiment did not show that AI cannot perform Product Value Discovery.

Quite the opposite.

AI successfully handled substantial parts of the work: public research, persona inference, first-use simulation, product exploration, capability mapping, competitive reasoning, buyer shifts, and business-model generation.

But it also showed why simply asking an AI to “analyze this product” is not enough.

The answer depends heavily on whether the AI is looking at:

  • what the company says the product is,
  • what users actually discover,
  • or what the product is technically capable of becoming.

Those three views can produce very different pictures of the same product, and very different opportunity maps when strategy is built from them.

For product teams entering a new market, that distinction matters.

The question is not only whether the product contains value.

It is whether the intended users can discover it, whether the company is emphasizing the right part of it, and whether another commercially stronger value is already present but effectively invisible.

AI can help answer all three.

But only if we are precise about which layer of value we are asking it to observe.
