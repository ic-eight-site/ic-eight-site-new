What Happens to AI Products When Users Get Better at AI?

Published August 2026

TL;DR: Some AI products may be valuable today partly because users still need help turning AI capability into usable workflows. But as users become better at giving AI context, defining outcomes, evaluating results, and delegating multi-step work, that value can shrink even if the product itself does not change. The competitive threat is not only better models. It is better users.

Some AI products may not be displaced by better models, but by users who no longer need their help.

Many AI products are built around a simple promise: they make an AI capability easier to use.

Instead of asking a general-purpose AI to interpret a goal, choose an approach, perform several steps, and revise the result, the product turns the task into a defined workflow. Upload this. Choose that. Click generate. Review the output.

That can be valuable.

But it may also depend on a temporary condition: the user does not yet know how to get the same outcome from a general-purpose AI.

The important shift is not only that general-purpose AI gets better.

The user gets better too.

As people become more accustomed to giving AI source material, explaining what they want, evaluating the result, asking for corrections, and delegating multi-step work, the value of some specialized interfaces may change even if the products themselves do not.

A product does not always lose value because a competitor improves.

Sometimes the user simply stops needing the layer of assistance the product was built to provide.

Better Users Are Not Prompt Experts

This does not require everyone to become a prompt engineer.

In practice, becoming better at using AI may mean something much simpler.

The user learns that they can provide a document instead of manually extracting information from it. They learn that they can describe the desired outcome instead of selecting every intermediate operation. They learn that a weak first result is not necessarily the end of the task because they can point out what is wrong and ask the AI to try again.

Eventually, the interaction becomes less about operating software correctly and more about directing work.

That is a meaningful change.

A user who once needed a specialized interface because they did not know how to structure the task may later find that the interface introduces more structure than they need.

The software has not become worse.

The user's alternative has become better.

This Is Already Happening Outside AI Products

The same shift can appear in ordinary software.

Knowledge-management tools traditionally assume that users will maintain structures so information can be found again: folders, databases, tags, titles, search fields, and other forms of organization.

But if AI can retrieve the relevant material from a collection based on a natural-language request, some of that preparatory work becomes less necessary.

The user may no longer need to remember where something was stored or what it was called.

They only need to describe what they are looking for.

The same is true of small utilities.

A user who once searched for an application that performed a particular task may now ask an AI to perform the task directly. Even the ability to generate a custom application does not necessarily solve this, because the user may not want software at all. They may only want the result.

This changes what counts as competition.

The alternative to a product is no longer always another product.

Sometimes it is simply asking AI.

The Question Is Not Whether General AI Can Technically Do It

This distinction matters because capability comparisons alone can be misleading.

A general-purpose AI may technically be capable of producing an image, analyzing a dataset, restructuring a document, editing code, or generating an application.

That does not automatically make a specialized product unnecessary.

The useful question is narrower:

Is this product solving the user's actual problem, or is it solving the temporary difficulty of using AI to solve that problem?

Those are different kinds of value.

If the product primarily removes the need to know how to direct AI, that value may shrink as users become more capable.

If the product contributes something that remains difficult to reproduce through general-purpose AI, its value may be more durable.

That could be a specialized capability, proprietary data, reliable execution, domain-specific controls, persistent workflow state, stronger data boundaries, or something else that matters to the task itself rather than to the user's unfamiliarity with AI.

The distinction is not between products that survive and products that disappear.

It is between value that depends on the user's current limitations and value that survives when those limitations change.

Product Value Can Move Without the Product Moving

This creates an unusual problem for AI products.

Traditional product evaluation often treats the product and its user as relatively stable objects. The product changes through releases. The competitive landscape changes as other companies launch new features.

But AI changes the user too.

A person who spends a year working with general-purpose AI may approach the same product very differently from someone encountering AI for the first time.

Their expectations change.

Their ability to delegate changes.

Their tolerance for predefined workflows changes.

Their understanding of what can be done without dedicated software changes.

The product may be identical.

The value discovered by the user is not.

This means some AI products face a form of competition that conventional competitive analysis can miss.

Their users are learning.

A Question for Product Value Evaluation

This has started to become relevant across Product Value Evaluations.

PVE already asks whether the value a company intends to offer is the value a user actually discovers through direct product use.

But AI products may require another question:

Does this product create value that survives when the user no longer needs help using AI?

That question cannot be answered from positioning alone.

A product may appear differentiated until it is used alongside the alternatives available to an experienced AI user. Another may initially look narrow, yet prove valuable because it takes responsibility for a part of the task that general-purpose AI does not handle well.

The point is not to assume that specialized AI products are temporary.

It is to identify what part of their value is temporary.

Because the next source of competitive pressure may not come from another startup or a better foundation model.

Not just from better models.

From better users.
