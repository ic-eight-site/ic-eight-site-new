Diagnosis & Priority

When Quality Becomes the Wrong Goal

A manager once told me 75 percent was enough. It took years to understand why he was right. · Published July 2026

Early in my career, while working in-house at a global technology company, I once stayed at my desk until around 10:30 p.m. reviewing a set of Japanese product manuals.

A manager noticed and asked why I was still there.

I said the work was not finished.

He told me that we did not need to produce something that was 95 percent perfect. Seventy-five percent was enough. What mattered to the customer was that the complete manual set existed in Japanese, was readable, and was available when people needed it.

At the time, I disagreed.

If we could produce something better, why would we deliberately release something worse?

It took me a while to understand that I was looking at the wrong thing.

I was looking at the quality of the output. He was looking at the value of the outcome.

Those are not always the same thing.

Why are we doing this?

Over the years, I found myself asking a version of the same question more often: Why are we doing this? What does this requirement actually improve? Is this detail important because it changes something for the customer, or because the people responsible for it have learned to treat it as important?

Specialists naturally become highly sensitive to the things they work on. Content teams see problems in content. Designers see problems in design. SEO specialists see problems in search visibility. Every discipline develops standards, conventions, and ideas about what good work looks like.

That expertise is useful. But it creates a risk.

The ability to identify an imperfection can easily become an assumption that the imperfection is worth fixing.

It may not be.

The question is not whether it could be better

A product or piece of content can already be clear, usable, and fit for purpose at 75 percent quality. Raising it to 95 percent may make it more polished, more consistent, or more elegant. But that does not automatically mean it creates enough additional value to justify the additional effort and cost.

The question is not whether 95 percent is better than 75 percent.

Of course it is.

The question is whether the difference matters enough to pay for.

There is a cost to quality that tends to disappear from conversations about quality itself.

Every additional review cycle, specialist, custom asset, adjustment, and refinement consumes time and money. Those resources could also be used elsewhere.

So "higher quality is better" is not enough to make a business case.

The useful question is not whether something could be better.

Almost everything could be better.

The useful question is what changes if it becomes better.

Does the improvement change what the customer can do? Does it reduce meaningful friction? Does it improve an outcome that matters? Does it create enough additional value to justify the additional cost?

If not, the improvement may still be desirable.

It is simply not a priority.

What I understand now

This is a distinction I did not understand early in my career. I thought my job was to produce the best possible output within my area of responsibility.

Over time, I came to see the limitation in that view.

The quality of a deliverable is not the same thing as the value it creates.

And the fact that something can be improved does not mean improving it is the best use of time, money, or attention.

That distinction now sits at the center of how I think about product work.

The goal is not to make every surface perfect.

The goal is to understand which improvements actually change the outcome.
