Diagnosis & Priority

Sudowrite Could Write the Prose. The Harder Part Was Building the Story.

Product Value Evaluation #044 · Published September 2026

Sudowrite is built specifically for fiction. That makes its value proposition more demanding than simply generating readable prose. General-purpose AI can already brainstorm premises, continue a paragraph, rewrite a scene, or produce several thousand words of fiction. A dedicated fiction product has to make some part of the harder work easier: maintaining story structure, developing a premise, managing continuity, or turning an idea into a usable draft without requiring the writer to design the story first.

This evaluation tested that boundary in Japanese. The initial task was intentionally simple: provide a minimal short-story premise and stylistic constraints, then let Sudowrite do as much of the plotting and drafting as possible.

P01 began from a minimal seed: every morning, at the same time, a sheet of paper describing “yesterday’s events” arrives in a man’s mailbox, although he has not yet experienced those events. Sudowrite was asked to write in Japanese, keep the protagonist unnamed, use distant third-person narration, let observable events carry emotion, restrain figurative language, and invent the plot itself. No sequence of events or ending was supplied. Because the temporal relationship implied by “yesterday” was later judged ambiguous, this run was not used as the strongest evidence for causal plotting performance.

The result separated two capabilities that can look similar from the outside. Sudowrite could generate substantial, fluent Japanese prose through its direct First Draft route. But across the natural first-use path and later controlled tests, it did not reliably turn either a minimal premise or an explicitly supplied causal structure into a coherent short story. The strongest value demonstrated in this evaluation was prose production inside a fiction-specific workspace, not autonomous plotting.

Key Findings

  • The natural first-use path did not reach a usable plot or draft. The Japanese premise remained in Story Bible, but the first generated Synopsis was an English paraphrase rather than a developed story, and no visible plot, Scenes, or Japanese prose appeared before the bounded session ended.
  • A later direct First Draft route demonstrated that Japanese generation itself was not the main problem. Sudowrite repeatedly produced substantial, readable Japanese prose, preserved the projects, tracked credit use, and supported .docx export.
  • Cleaner prompts did not reliably solve story construction. In the photograph tests, explicit temporal, genre, and ending constraints were still violated or left unresolved.
  • The strongest controlled test supplied a familiar causal skeleton rather than asking Sudowrite to invent a new narrative structure. The generated story still failed to make the protagonist’s avoidance action plausibly cause the predicted outcome.
  • Across the follow-up runs, more and more of the plotting work moved back to the evaluator: first clarifying time mechanics, then changing the narrative device, then specifying genre and payoff rules, and finally supplying the causal architecture itself. The tested workflow therefore did not demonstrate that Sudowrite reduced plotting work for this short-story use case.

Evaluation Setup

The persona was a short-fiction writer who wanted help developing a story rather than merely expanding an already completed plot. The writer preferred an anonymous protagonist, third-person narrative distance, events carrying emotional meaning rather than explicit psychological explanation, restrained figurative language, and a compact short-story form.

The frozen question was whether Sudowrite could turn a minimal Japanese seed into a coherent short story without requiring the writer to supply the plot. “Interesting” was part of the original framing, but taste is too subjective to serve as the decisive test. The evaluation therefore separated four questions: did Sudowrite interpret the input correctly, did it construct a causal plot, did it reduce the amount of story design the writer had to supply, and did it generate usable Japanese prose?

Across the follow-up tests, the experiment deliberately narrowed what Sudowrite had to invent. The aim was not to make each prompt progressively easier until it passed, but to identify where the failure moved as ambiguity and structural freedom were removed.

The initial natural first-use session used a fresh Free Trial account in Chrome and lasted roughly 14 minutes. Later direct-generation runs were deliberately kept separate from that first-use record because they used different entry points, additional instructions, revised premises, or more explicit causal constraints.

The Natural First-Use Path Stopped Before the Story Appeared

The onboarding flow was clearly built around fiction rather than generic text generation. Sudowrite exposed Story Bible, Synopsis, Outline, POV, tense, Scenes, Draft, and other story-oriented controls. The Japanese premise stayed intact when entered into the project.

The first visible generated artifact, however, was a Synopsis in English. It mostly paraphrased the supplied premise rather than developing a sequence of events or an ending.

Further attempts to continue through the structural workflow did not produce a visible plot or scene sequence within the session. The editor still showed zero words at the stopping point. A scene-free Draft route then warned that results could be unexpected and estimated a cost of 6,500–13,000 credits, while the trial had 9,391 credits remaining. The draft was not run from that state.

This does not establish that nothing happened in the backend. A later activity ledger showed two charged Write Auto jobs whose outputs were not recovered. The narrower finding is that the natural first-use path did not surface usable story value within the bounded session.

That distinction matters. The product showed a fiction-specific operating model, but the writer had not yet received the thing that motivated the test: a story structure or draft that reduced the work of inventing the plot.

Direct First Draft Showed That Japanese Prose Was Not the Main Obstacle

The persona-free audit found a much more direct route. First Draft could produce substantial Japanese text without first completing the full Story Bible path.

One run generated 4,705 extracted Japanese characters and consumed 627 credits. Another generated 4,314 characters for 925 credits. Projects persisted, generation activity appeared in the ledger, and documents could be exported as .docx.

The Japanese itself was generally fluent and readable. Its default narrative register leaned more toward contemporary commercial fiction than toward restrained literary prose, but that is a question of stylistic fit rather than basic Japanese competence. The test did not support a claim that Sudowrite could not write Japanese.

The distinction became important because readable sentences could mask problems at the story level. The product could keep producing prose even when the temporal rules or causal structure underneath the prose had drifted.

In the clarified time-based run, for example, the prompt explicitly supplied the future-self relationship that had been ambiguous in the original premise. The resulting prose nevertheless collapsed the information back into the recipient’s already-lived past, rather than preserving the supplied temporal relationship. It also exceeded the requested compact length.

The writing pipeline worked. The story logic was less reliable.

The Photograph Tests Reduced Ambiguity but Still Required Repair

Because the original premise involved an ambiguous use of “yesterday,” it was not strong enough to support a hard failure claim. The next tests therefore replaced the message with a simpler device: photographs showing a future state.

The later First Draft tests used English instruction scaffolds with Japanese story seeds. In the first photograph test, the seed specified that one photograph appeared in the mailbox each morning, showing “the man” later that same night, with only that day’s date written on the reverse. Because the Japanese referred to “the man” rather than explicitly saying “himself,” the identity relationship was not treated as the strongest evidence of failure. Sudowrite still had to invent the source of the photographs, the causal sequence, and the ending.

The next version tightened the temporal rules, specified a short-mystery form, required a concrete factual reversal, and explicitly ruled out a self-discovery lesson or unexplained symbolic ending. It still left Sudowrite to determine what happened, why it happened, and how the ending followed from the earlier events.

Yet the first photograph run still drifted from its rules. A same-night mechanism shifted into daytime imagery, an emphasized repeated date was introduced without being causally resolved, and the ending remained abstract rather than making the setup concrete.

Even with those additions, the output violated the date-only rule by adding written messages to the photographs. The requested factual reversal did not arrive, and the story exceeded the requested length.

This progression mattered more than whether the individual stories were enjoyable. Each revision removed a possible excuse for the previous result. The prompt became cleaner, but the evaluator also had to do more of the structural work.

At that point, the question was no longer whether a more detailed prompt could eventually produce a usable story. It was whether Sudowrite was reducing the writer’s plotting burden or gradually returning it to the writer in the form of prompt engineering.

The Prophecy Test Isolated Causal Plotting

The final controlled test removed even more freedom.

The causal skeleton was deliberately familiar: an Oedipus-style structure in which the attempt to escape a prediction becomes the mechanism that fulfills it. Sudowrite did not need to invent a new narrative architecture. It only had to translate that recognizable structure into new characters, circumstances, and events.

The evaluator supplied the causal architecture, but not the prophecy, avoidance strategy, protagonist, setting, intermediate events, or revelation. A competent modern variation would have been enough. Originality was not the threshold; causal execution was.

This produced the clearest result of the evaluation.

The generated protagonist was told that he would remain at the bottom of the deep sea forever. His supposed attempt to avoid that fate was to go to sea. That is not a rational avoidance action. Later, he resurfaced and returned to a boat, so the prediction of remaining submerged forever was not fulfilled either. The narration repeatedly framed the events as an inescapable fulfillment of fate, but the events themselves did not establish the causal relationship the prose claimed. The generation then ended mid-sentence.

This was more revealing than a stylistic failure. Sudowrite was not being asked to invent a new plot archetype. It was being asked to preserve a familiar causal pattern while changing its surface details. Even with that structure already supplied, the generated events did not make the causal mechanism work.

The prose said the mechanism existed. The plot did not.

The Author Work Did Not Disappear

Across the experiment, the evaluator progressively supplied more of the work that the product was being tested to perform.

The first premise left a temporal relationship open. That was clarified. The narrative device was then replaced with photographs to reduce ambiguity. Temporal and genre constraints were added. A concrete payoff was requested. Finally, the entire causal skeleton of the story was supplied.

At each stage, Sudowrite continued to produce prose. But the amount of human specification required to make the plotting test cleaner increased.

For this use case, that matters more than prose volume.

A writer who already knows the scenes, actions, and ending may encounter a different value proposition. Sudowrite’s fiction-specific workspace, persistent projects, direct drafting, rewrite tools, and long-form structure could plausibly make it useful as an expansion layer once the story is already substantially designed.

That workflow was not tested here.

What was tested was a writer who wanted the product to take a premise and assume much of the plotting work. On that task, the value was not demonstrated.

Implications

Sudowrite’s clearest differentiation from a generic chatbot is not that it can generate fiction. Generic models can already do that. Its more defensible proposition is the connected fiction environment around the generation: persistent projects, Story Bible, structure, scenes, specialized drafting and revision tools, and a workflow intended to keep fiction context together.

Parts of that proposition were visible in this evaluation. Project persistence worked. Japanese First Draft generation worked. Export worked. Credit accounting was visible.

Fiction specialization was clearly visible in the workspace and generation workflow, but specialization alone did not translate into reliable causal story construction in this evaluation.

The more important question is whether those components reduce the hard work of fiction rather than simply provide a specialized interface for generating prose.

For the tested short-story workflow, the answer remained unresolved on structure and negative on autonomous plotting. Sudowrite generated the surface of stories more reliably than it generated their causal machinery.

That is not the same as saying the product cannot write fiction. It suggests a narrower distinction: in this evaluation, producing prose was easier for Sudowrite than building the story the prose was supposed to enact.

Evaluation Limits

This was a simulated first-use evaluation, not recruited-user research.

P01 lasted roughly 14 minutes and ended before a visible plot, Scenes sequence, or Japanese draft appeared. Later First Draft tests used a different route and therefore cannot be used to retroactively mark the original first-use path successful.

The original “yesterday” premise contained genuine temporal ambiguity and was not used as the strongest evidence of failure. The photograph prompts reduced that ambiguity but were not perfectly specification-proof either. The strongest causal evidence came from the prophecy test because the required mechanism was explicit.

No matched generic-LLM comparison was run. The evaluation therefore does not establish that a generic model would have performed better or worse under identical conditions.

Long-form continuity, Muse, Style Examples, Plugins, Canvas, collaboration, paid-plan behavior, and scene-to-prose expansion from a fully developed outline were not tested.

Credit use also varied considerably across runs. The 10,000-credit trial ended with 3,761 credits after the recorded tests, but the small sample does not support a stable per-story cost estimate.

Conclusion

No claim is made that Sudowrite cannot write fiction, or that it would perform worse than a generic model under identical conditions.

Within the boundaries tested, Sudowrite reliably produced fluent Japanese prose inside a fiction-specific workspace. It did not reliably build the causal story that prose is supposed to enact, even after the evaluator progressively supplied clearer time mechanics, a simpler narrative device, explicit genre and payoff rules, and finally the entire causal skeleton. Each step moved the plotting burden back toward the writer rather than away from it.

Finding the gap is only half the work. The other half is deciding what to do about it.
